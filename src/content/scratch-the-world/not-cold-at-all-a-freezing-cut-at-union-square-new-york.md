On Monday, 27 November 2017, I set a battery-powered Numark PT01 Scratch down on the south steps of Union Square in Manhattan and cut with the crew in the biting late-autumn cold. My caption that day was pure bravado — "Not cold at all" — typed with numb fingers while the wind came straight down 14th Street. Here is what it is like to scratch a portable turntable in New York's most democratic public square: the greenmarket, the Washington statue, the chess tables, and how to play there without getting moved along.

## The square named for a meeting of roads

Union Square is not named for the Civil War Union, the way half the tourists assume. It takes its name from a far older "union" — the point where Broadway and the old Bowery road once converged. The city laid it out as a public place in 1839 and opened it as a park in 1845, and from almost the beginning it has been New York's town hall without a roof. The first Labor Day parade in the country stepped off here in 1882, and ever since the square has been the default stage for free-speech rallies, protests and demonstrations. Standing in the middle of it with a turntable, you feel that history in the geography: the six-and-a-half-acre square sits right where Flatiron to the north, Greenwich Village to the west and the East Village to the east all run into one another, so the crowd is never one kind of New Yorker.

## Washington on his horse, Lincoln in the trees

The park is anchored by nineteenth-century bronze. On the south side a massive equestrian statue of George Washington has reared over the plaza since 1856 — the second equestrian statue ever raised in the United States — and further in you find Abraham Lincoln and the Marquis de Lafayette standing among the bare November trees. Across 14th Street, the south face of the square is dominated by the Metronome, that huge digital-clock art installation whose numbers most people have long given up trying to read. The square is boxed in by 14th Street on the south, 17th Street on the north, and Union Square West and East down either flank, and underneath all of it roars the 14th Street–Union Square station, pumping the 4, 5, 6, L, N, Q, R and W trains' worth of commuters straight up into the plaza. You are never short of an audience here; you are only ever short of one that is standing still.

## A Monday greenmarket in the cold

I had picked a Monday, which meant the Union Square Greenmarket was running — it sets up four days a week, Mondays among them — so the north end of the square was a grid of white tents. Late November turns the stalls into a harvest still life: crates of root vegetables, the last storage apples, jugs of cloudy cider, bundled evergreens and dried wreaths for the season. The whole square smelled of it — pine and damp soil off the market, caramelised sugar from a roasting-nut cart, exhaust drifting in off 14th Street — all of it sharpened by the cold. The light was the giveaway that it was nearly December: a thin, low-angled sun slipping between the masonry high-rises, so that half the plaza glowed pale gold while the other half had already sunk into blue shadow, and everybody's breath hung in front of them.

## Cutting on the south steps with the crew

The wide tiered stone steps at the south end are the social heart of Union Square, and that is where we set up. I was not alone — this was a session with the crew, Marc and the others, trading the fader back and forth and heckling each other between cuts. Around us the usual Union Square cast carried on: skateboarders bailing tricks off the perimeter ledges, a knot of chess players hunched over the concrete tables deeper in the park, a guitarist somewhere off to the west. The soundtrack was all New York — the L train rumbling shallow and close under our feet, sirens running down 14th Street, the crack of boards slapping concrete. "Not cold at all," I wrote, which was a lie; within half an hour my fingers had gone stiff on the Mixfader and I was tucking a hand into my sleeve between records. But that is the thing nobody tells you about cutting outside in the cold with friends: the laughing keeps you warmer than the gloves would.

## Where a portable turntable is allowed here

Union Square is about the friendliest place in the city to do this, and it helps to know why. Performing, speaking and playing music in a traditional public park like this one is protected expression under long-standing New York law, so you are not sneaking around — you have a right to be there. The line the Parks rules actually care about is amplification: large, commercial, AC-powered amplifiers and generators are what trigger a permit requirement and a complaint, while a small battery-powered rig like the PT01 on its own little speaker sits safely inside protected, acoustic-scale performance. The rest is spatial etiquette. You keep clear of the ADA ramps, you never block a subway stairwell or a greenmarket tent, and you stay out of the main commuter lanes. Set up on the open pavers of the south plaza, a step off the direct flow, and you can cut all afternoon without a Parks Enforcement Patrol officer ever needing to wander over.

## Frequently asked questions

### Where did you scratch on 27 November 2017?

In Union Square, in Manhattan — on the south plaza and the wide stone steps at the 14th Street end, under the George Washington statue, with the greenmarket tents at the north end and the Metronome clock across the street.

### Do you need a permit to scratch in Union Square?

For a small battery-powered turntable played on its own speaker, generally no — that counts as protected, acoustic-scale performance in a New York City park. The moment you bring a commercial AC-powered amplifier or a generator, you cross into permit territory and risk a complaint. Either way you are expected to keep clear of ADA ramps, subway stairwells, the greenmarket tents and the main pedestrian lanes. Check the current NYC Parks rules before you go.

### Isn't late November far too cold to scratch outside?

It is genuinely cold — my fingers were stiff on the fader inside half an hour and the wind funnels right down 14th Street. But doing it with the crew is the whole point. You pass the turntable around, you laugh at how ridiculous it is to be out there, and the session keeps you warmer than standing still ever would.

### What gear did you use?

A Numark PT01 Scratch — a portable, battery-powered turntable — paired with a wireless Mixfader. The whole rig fits in a shoulder bag and sets up on a park step in under a minute.
