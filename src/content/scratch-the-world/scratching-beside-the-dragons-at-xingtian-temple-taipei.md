On 6 September 2017 I set my battery-powered Numark PT01 down near the forecourt of Xingtian Temple (行天宮), on the corner of Songjiang Road and Minquan East Road in Taipei's Zhongshan District, and cut a short session with the temple's carved dragons and qilin looming over my shoulder. The caption I posted was only five words — "Scratching with Chinese Dragon and Kylin" — because the backdrop really did say everything. Five days after the dancers' plaza at Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hall, this was a completely different kind of stage: quieter, older, and watched over by a god of merchants. Here's what it's like to bring a turntable to one of Taipei's most solemn and busiest temples.

## The smoke-free temple of Guan Gong

Xingtian Temple is dedicated to Guan Gong (關聖帝君) — Guan Yu, the red-faced general of the Three Kingdoms era who was later deified as a patron of loyalty, courage and, very fittingly for this business-heavy corner of the city, merchants and money. That's a big part of why it's one of Taipei's most heavily visited temples: people come to settle contracts, careers and frayed nerves with him.

The thing that surprises every first-time visitor, though, is what's *missing*. In August 2014 the temple swept away almost all the usual paraphernalia of Taiwanese worship: it banned the burning of incense, banned joss (spirit) paper, and even hauled out the big tables that normally groan with food offerings. Instead of the thick, eye-watering smoke you get at most temples, worshippers here are asked to bring only sincerity and pray with their palms pressed together. Standing on the forecourt without that haze, everything felt unusually sharp — sunlight bouncing off the brilliant green glazed roof tiles and the deep vermilion gates, and, honestly, air clean enough to actually think about where to drop a needle.

## Dragons, qilin, and a five-word caption

Because there's no smoke to squint through, your eyes go straight up to the roofline. Elaborate, brightly painted dragons coil along the ridges, and qilin — those hooved, mythical chimeras that stand for prosperity and calm — keep watch over the courtyard, framing a clean rectangle of Taipei sky. That is the entire reason my caption was so short. "Scratching with Chinese Dragon and Kylin" wasn't me being lazy; it was me pointing a thumb over my shoulder at a pair of guardians who had stood there for decades while I added a few seconds of crossfader chika-chika underneath them.

## Blue robes and whispered prayers

Even without offerings, the temple absolutely hums. The clearest sign of that is the shou-jing (收驚) ritual — literally "collecting fright," a spirit-calming rite. All day long, queues of stressed office workers, suited types straight off the MRT, and mums bouncing restless babies wait their turn for the xiaolaosheng (效勞生): volunteers in striking bright-blue Daoist robes who trace slow, deliberate circles with a single stick of the only incense the temple still permits, coaxing a startled or scattered soul back into the body.

Against that backdrop — an ocean of shuffling feet and the low, collective murmur of thousands of half-whispered prayers — a portable turntable has to know its place. I kept my cuts low and let them fold into the roar of scooters pouring down Minquan East Road rather than competing with anything happening in the courtyard. A DJ here is a guest, not the main event.

## Keeping a respectful distance

This is not a spot where you set up in front of the main gates and let rip. Xingtian Temple is solemn, orderly and pointedly non-commercial, and blasting music at the entrance would be flat-out disrespectful — worse, it could cut across the quiet shou-jing rituals going on just inside. The unwritten etiquette for any street musician here is to keep a respectful distance from the doors: drift toward the MRT exits (Xingtian Temple Station, Exit 3) or the ordinary commercial sidewalks a little way off, keep the volume modest, and let your sound blend into the city instead of slicing through the prayers.

So that's exactly what I did. I balanced the PT01 on a low ledge well clear of the worship hall, dropped a 7-inch, and treated it as a private, almost invisible jam — no PA, no hat out for money, just a battery-powered turntable and the mythical creatures on the roof for company.

## The fortune-tellers under the street

One of my favourite details here isn't the temple at all — it's what's under the road. Take the stairs down into the pedestrian underpass beneath Songjiang Road and you land in 命理街, Fortune-Telling Street: a low, neon-lit warren of stalls doing palmistry, face-reading and even bird divination, all quietly feeding off the temple's endless foot traffic. It's the spiritual commerce that isn't allowed upstairs, spilling out into the infrastructure instead.

Standing between the smoke-free courtyard above and the fortune-tellers below, the whole neighbourhood felt like it ran on one current: people coming to Guan Gong to settle something inside themselves — whether through a blue-robed volunteer, a fortune-teller's booth, or, that particular afternoon, a DJ cutting a record.

## Why the temple was the right stop

Scratch the World has always been about carrying a turntable into ordinary, unexpected, in-between places and leaving a little sound behind. A working temple watched over by a god of merchants — where the air is deliberately kept clear and the loudest thing in the courtyard is a whisper — is about as far from a nightclub as you can get, and that's precisely why it earns a pin on the map. Everything I needed still fit in one bag over a shoulder. Three stops into Taiwan, the run was starting to feel less like a novelty and more like a real map of home.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can you busk or play music at Xingtian Temple?

Not right at the gates. Xingtian Temple is a solemn, orderly, non-commercial place of worship, and loud music by the main entrance would be disrespectful and could disturb the shou-jing rituals happening inside. The accepted etiquette for street musicians is to keep a respectful distance — nearer the MRT exits or the commercial sidewalks away from the doors — keep the volume modest, and let the sound blend into the street. I kept my session quiet, well clear of the worship hall, with no PA and no tip jar.

### What is Xingtian Temple?

It's one of Taipei's busiest temples, in Zhongshan District at the corner of Songjiang Road and Minquan East Road, dedicated to Guan Gong (Guan Yu) — the deified Three Kingdoms general worshipped as a patron of loyalty, courage and merchants. Since August 2014 it has banned incense and joss-paper burning and removed its offering tables, so people worship simply, with pressed palms. It has its own MRT station, Xingtian Temple, with Exit 3 letting out right by the grounds.

### Why doesn't the temple allow incense?

In August 2014 Xingtian Temple adopted a sweeping health-and-environment policy, ending the burning of incense and spirit paper and taking away the food-offering tables to cut smoke and pollution. Worshippers are asked to bring only sincerity. The result is a rare, almost smoke-free calm compared with most Taiwanese temples — and it's a big part of why the courtyard feels so visually crisp, with the green roof tiles and red gates popping in the clear air.

### What gear did you use?

A Numark PT01 Scratch — a battery-powered portable turntable built for scratch DJs — with a portable fader and a 7-inch scratch record. Because it runs on batteries and packs into a shoulder bag, it sets up anywhere, which is the only reason a quiet session beside a Taipei temple is even possible.
