On a strange, shifting December afternoon in 2017, I set a battery-powered Numark PT01 Scratch down at 34th Street–Hudson Yards, on the far west edge of Manhattan, and cut records with the half-built towers and the rising Vessel looming behind the construction fences. Here is what it is really like to scratch a portable turntable in the rawest, newest, windiest corner of New York.

My caption that day was short and stubborn: "Strange weather but it can't stop me." It was the kind of early-December sky that cannot decide what it is — pale sun one minute, a flat grey lid the next, and a wind off the Hudson that went straight through everything. I had come out to the Far West Side on purpose, to the one part of Manhattan that in late 2017 still felt genuinely unfinished, and I pulled the turntable out of my bag on a plaza that was being poured into existence around me.

## The far west edge of Manhattan, still under construction

In December 2017, Hudson Yards was the biggest open wound and the biggest promise in the city at the same time. The whole district was rising on a deck being built over the West Side rail yards, and from street level it was all plywood hoardings, chain-link, and the orange-and-white barrels that funnel you around a site. Behind the fences the towers were going up floor by floor — the first of them already occupied, the rest still steel skeletons wrapped in scaffolding and crane booms. Somewhere in the middle of it, the lattice honeycomb of the Vessel was only just climbing out of the ground, nowhere near the copper-coloured landmark it would become. You could stand on 10th or 11th Avenue and watch a neighbourhood being invented in real time, and it made for a very odd stage: half building site, half brand-new corporate plaza, with almost nothing soft anywhere in the frame.

## Up out of the newest station in the city

The 34th Street–Hudson Yards station is the youngest stop on the whole subway map — the lonely western end of the 7 line, opened only a couple of years before I turned up with my records. You come up out of it on long, deep escalators under a sweeping glass canopy that still looked like an architect's render, and the ride up spits you out into the open with the wind already waiting. On a cold weekday afternoon the flow is strange: a thin trickle of commuters surfacing and immediately ducking their chins into their collars, hurrying toward the few finished office lobbies, and then long stretches where the plaza is simply empty. For a street performer that emptiness cuts both ways — nobody tells you to move along, but nobody is standing still long enough to become a crowd, either.

## Finding a windbreak on Hudson Boulevard

The real enemy out there is not the cold so much as the wind. It funnels off the river and accelerates between the half-built towers, and it grabs at everything — the hoardings rattle, your jacket snaps, and the edge of a seven-inch record lifts right off the platter if you let it. I tucked myself into the lee of a fresh granite planter along Hudson Boulevard, using the new landscaping as a windbreak, and set the PT01 on the flattest ledge I could find. In that cold the plastic chassis goes stiff and a little brittle, and your fingers stop cooperating within a few minutes. Fast flares and crabs are the first thing to go when you can't feel your hands, so I leaned into what still worked: heavier, slower tears, deliberate baby scratches, and a lot of shielding the platter with a gloved hand so a gust couldn't send the needle skating across a DJ Premier or Mobb Deep instrumental.

## Scratching against a construction symphony

There is no such thing as a quiet take at Hudson Yards. Whatever I pushed out of the little speaker had to fight an entire industrial orchestra: the staccato hammer of a jackhammer, the slow hydraulic groan of a crane lifting another I-beam, the clang of the site and the general low roar drifting up from the direction of the Javits Center. Oddly, that competition is good for the music — boom-bap is built out of exactly those textures, and cutting a drum break against real jackhammers makes the whole thing feel less like a performance and more like it belongs there. The audience, when there was one, split neatly into two tribes: office early-adopters in good coats cutting across the plaza without breaking stride, and crews of construction workers in neon hi-vis, hard hats pushed back, eating lunch on stacks of drywall and sheet material. The hard hats were the better crowd every time.

## A ghost of old New York in a brand-new plaza

What I liked about the spot was how caught-in-between it felt. Turn one way and there was the dormant northern spur of the High Line curling in over the streets, and beyond it the long cold ribbon of Hudson River Park running up the water's edge — the gritty, weathered, slightly wild Manhattan I grew up loving. Turn the other way and there was this hyper-polished, ultra-corporate plaza still in its awkward infancy, granite and glass and not a scuff on any of it. Standing in the seam between the two with a portable turntable, scratching old vinyl in the cold, I felt like a small stubborn ghost of old New York who had wandered into a boardroom by mistake. Strange weather, strange place — but the records still turned, and that was the whole point.

## Frequently asked questions

### Where did you scratch on 6 December 2017?

At 34th Street–Hudson Yards on Manhattan's Far West Side — out on the new granite plazas around Hudson Boulevard and the 7-train terminal, with the half-built Hudson Yards towers and the rising Vessel behind the construction fences, the High Line's northern spur and Hudson River Park off to the west.

### Isn't an open, half-built plaza a terrible place to scratch in winter?

It is genuinely rough — the wind comes straight off the Hudson and accelerates between the towers, your fingers go numb in minutes, and gusts try to lift the record off the platter. The trick is to tuck into a windbreak, shield the platter with a gloved hand, and swap fast flares and crabs for heavier, slower cuts you can still land with stiff fingers.

### How do you keep a portable turntable playing in strong wind?

Set up behind something solid — a planter, a wall, a ledge — so the gusts don't hit the deck head-on, keep the tonearm weight honest, and be ready to steady the edge of the record with your free hand. A small battery rig like the PT01 is light, which is exactly why the wind notices it.

### What gear did you use?

A Numark PT01 Scratch — a portable, battery-powered turntable — paired with a wireless Mixfader. The whole rig fits in a shoulder bag and sets up on a ledge in under a minute, which is what makes a spot like this possible at all.
