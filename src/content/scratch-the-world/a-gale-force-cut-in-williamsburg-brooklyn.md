Short answer: yes, you can scratch outdoors in a gale — but the wind will do its best to carry your gear off with it. On 5 January 2018, in the raw tail of the storm that had just battered the East Coast, I set a battery turntable down on the Williamsburg waterfront in Brooklyn and watched the wind off the East River tear my slipmat clean off the platter. Here is what portable turntablism actually feels like when the weather is trying to end your set.

## The storm I had no business walking into

Early January 2018 was not the week to be carrying a turntable outdoors. New York was still picking itself up from the brutal cold snap that had slammed the whole coast a day earlier — that bomb-cyclone kind of cold that cracks your lips, turns slush to grey iron underfoot, and empties the sidewalks of everyone with any sense. The sensible plan was to stay in with a cup of tea and a pair of headphones. Instead I zipped the Numark PT01 into a shoulder bag, pulled my hood tight, and rode the L train out to Williamsburg anyway, because the Brooklyn side of the East River is the best winter backdrop in the city — and on a day like this, I knew I would have the whole waterfront to myself.

## Standing on the edge of the world

If you walk the North Williamsburg waterfront in January, you quickly learn there is nothing between you and the river. Down at the old East River State Park the ground is just broad concrete slabs and a few stretches of rusted rail track left over from the neighbourhood's industrial years, laid open to a flawless, cinematic wall of Manhattan across the water. A few blocks south, Domino Park did not exist yet — in early 2018 it was still a fenced-off construction site along Kent Avenue, and the enormous rusting skeleton of the old Domino Sugar Refinery loomed over the whole scene like something out of a dystopian film. It is a staggering place to stand with a turntable. It is also, I would find out within minutes, a completely unsheltered one.

## When the gale took my slipmat

Here is the thing nobody warns you about the Williamsburg waterfront in winter: the East River is a wind tunnel. The gusts come howling off the concrete canyons of Manhattan, pick up speed across the open water with nothing to slow them, and slam straight into the Brooklyn shoreline without a single tree or building to break them first. These are not polite breezes — they come up off the water in chaotic, lifting updrafts, and the moment I set the deck down on a ledge and dropped the needle, one of them reached under the lip of the record and simply took my slipmat. It lifted off the platter and went cartwheeling away down the waterfront, gone for good. As I wrote under the clip that day, it flew off "like your EX — never comes back." After that it was me against the physics: tucking my body and the flap of my coat around the turntable as a human windbreak, fighting just to keep the tonearm from skating clean across the wax every time a fresh gust came through.

## A blurry clip I refused to shoot twice

The video I posted from that session is, by my own honest admission, a mess — shaky, grainy, the frame juddering every time the wind shoved the camera. Normally I would reset and shoot it again until it looked clean. Not that day. "I know the video is blurry," I wrote, "I don't wanna do it again though." And I meant it. There is a point in a cold like that where the realness of the moment matters more than the polish — numb fingers, a lost slipmat, a half-frozen crossfader hand that will not stay loose enough for clean chirps, and a record that keeps trying to jump the groove. A tidy re-take would have been a lie. The blurry one was the truth of what it took to land even a few bars out there.

## Who stops to listen in a windstorm

You do not draw a crowd on the Williamsburg waterfront in a January gale, and that is the honest truth of it. In that kind of cold, the city's buskers are smart — most of them have long since retreated down into the warm tiled belly of the Bedford Avenue L platform to play where the wind cannot reach. Setting up outdoors in that weather is a genuine anomaly, and nobody lingers; it is simply too cold to stand still. What you get instead is the quieter currency of the street: a silent nod from someone hustling past with their head down, a phone lifted for a quick three-second clip, a knowing look from a local who clocks exactly how stupid and how dedicated you have to be to drag a turntable out into that. A compact battery deck like the PT01 is small enough that nobody official cares either — no permit, no hassle from the precinct, as long as you keep your footprint to the size of the turntable and stay out of everybody's way. On a day like that, three strangers slowing down was a standing ovation.

## Frequently asked questions

### Where exactly was this scratch session?

On the North Williamsburg waterfront in Brooklyn, around the East River State Park end of Kent Avenue, looking across the water at the Manhattan skyline with the old Domino Sugar Refinery cranes standing over the river behind me. In early 2018 the land that is now Domino Park was still a construction site.

### Did the wind really blow your slipmat away?

It really did. The East River waterfront acts like a wind tunnel in winter, and one hard gust got under the edge of the record and lifted the slipmat straight off the platter — it went tumbling off down the waterfront and I never got it back. After that I used my own body and coat as a windbreak just to keep the needle in the groove.

### How do you keep a portable turntable playing in high wind?

You stop fighting the wind head-on and start blocking it. I turn my back and shoulders into the gusts and curl my coat around the deck as a shield, keep the setup low on a solid ledge so it cannot get knocked, and lean on heavier tears and slow baby scratches instead of fast flares — the cuts you can still land when your hand is stiff and the platter is getting buffeted.

### What gear did you use?

A Numark PT01 Scratch — the battery-powered portable turntable I take everywhere — paired with a Jesse Dean JDDX2R crossfader, the fader that had already proved it could survive this cold snap a few days earlier. The whole rig lives in a shoulder bag and sets up on a ledge in under a minute, which is the only reason taking it out into a Brooklyn windstorm is even thinkable.
