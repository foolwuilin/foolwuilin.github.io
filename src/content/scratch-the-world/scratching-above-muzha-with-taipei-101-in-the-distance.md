On 14 September 2017 I carried a battery-powered Numark PT01 up into the hills of Muzha (木柵), on the green southern rim of Taipei, and cut a few bars on a low wall while Taipei 101 rose straight out of the mountain ridgeline in the distance. This is what it's like to scratch a portable turntable on a tea-mountain hillside above the city — humid, half-hidden, and completely off the tourist script.

The caption I posted that day was half wonder and half confession: "Taipei 101, the building thrust out from the mountain XD… the sound of scratching is terrible. Gotta practice harder on a tiny point." Both halves were true. From up here the tower really does look planted in the forest, and my cuts that afternoon really were rough — a light portable deck on an uneven wall will humble you fast.

## The skyline that grows out of a mountain

Muzha, in Wenshan District, is a lush, hilly stretch on the southern rim of the Taipei basin, tea country long before it was gondola country. The magic of the view is really an optical illusion of geography. Looking north from the Maokong ridge, your eye crosses a run of rolling, jungle-green foothills that hide the flat sprawl of the city behind them — so the bamboo-stalk silhouette of Taipei 101 appears to shoot straight up out of the ridgeline, with the cluster of towers around the World Trade Center jutting up behind the hills as if they'd been dropped into a forest. That was the shot the caption was reaching for: not a skyscraper over a city, but a skyscraper growing out of a mountain.

## Getting up into Muzha and Maokong

Most people arrive at the bottom, at the sprawling, jungle-like Taipei Zoo, and ride the Zoo–Maokong Gondola as it sweeps quietly over the canopy. The hills themselves are strung along Section 3 of Zhinan Road, a narrow, winding mountain lane that threads past hillside trails and old tea farms, with the imposing 19th-century Zhinan Temple — a big Taoist sanctuary of stone stairways — prominent enough to have its own gondola stop. It's close enough to the MRT to be an easy afternoon, and remote enough that ten minutes up the slope the city stops feeling like a city at all.

## A hot, loud, green afternoon

September in these hills is thick. The humidity clings to your skin, tropical and damp, just a touch cooler than the concrete oven downtown, with the sky heavy enough to promise a late-afternoon thunderstorm. The air carries the earthy, roasted fragrance of Tieguanyin — Iron Goddess of Mercy tea — brewing in the open-air cafes, mixed with warm wet asphalt, damp moss and the two-stroke haze of 125cc scooters grinding up the inclines. Overhead the cicadas roar in deafening metallic waves that swell and fade, trading off against the rustle of bamboo. The light does the rest: harsh and bright by day, then a hazy golden hour where the moisture in the air catches the sun in a soft mist, just before the neon of the basin below starts to flicker on.

## Setting up the PT01 on a mountain wall

Up here you're on your own for power — there are no outdoor outlets on a tea trail, so the whole rig runs on batteries, deck and little speaker both. Zhinan Road is tight and largely sidewalk-less, its pavement shared between pedestrians, scooters and the odd sausage cart, so the first job is finding a flat perch that doesn't block anyone. I set the PT01 down, kept the internal speaker low — half out of respect, half because sound carries strangely on a quiet slope — and dropped the needle. And yes, exactly as the caption admitted, it was rough: the PT01's light platter and light tonearm punish anything aggressive, and on an uneven wall the needle wants to jump, so you scratch smaller, cleaner, chasing that "tiny point" the caption was chasing, aiming your cuts out at the horizon instead of at a crowd.

## Why a tea mountain earns a place on the map

Taipei has a genuinely organized street-performance culture: buskers audition for a street-artist (街頭藝人) permit and mostly work the flat, high-traffic zones — Xinyi's shopping district, the lanes of Ximending — with acoustic guitars and pop ballads. A turntable up in the Maokong hills is wildly off that script. The crowd here isn't a busking crowd at all: older locals out hiking, couples on quiet dates, families settled in for a sit-down tea dinner. Dropping a crossfader into a place people climb specifically to escape the noise of the city is a small, friendly rebellion — modern electronic culture set against ancient mountain quiet, a hip-hop scratch answered by the cicadas. That collision is the entire point of Scratch the World, and a tea mountain looking out at Taipei 101 turned out to be one of its better stages.

## Frequently asked questions

### Where was this filmed?

On a hillside up in Muzha (木柵), in Wenshan District on the southern edge of Taipei — the tea-mountain area around Maokong, above Taipei Zoo. From that height, looking north across the forested foothills, Taipei 101 lines up with the ridgeline in the distance and appears to rise straight out of the mountains.

### Can you really see Taipei 101 from Maokong and Muzha?

Yes — it's one of the classic Taipei views. Because the Maokong ridge looks out over rolling green foothills that hide the flat city behind them, the tower and the buildings around it seem to grow directly out of the ridgeline rather than stand in a built-up skyline. It's especially striking at dusk, when the city lights come up beneath the silhouette.

### Is street performing (busking) legal in Taipei?

Only in the right places and with the right paperwork. Since the early 2000s, Taipei's buskers (街頭藝人) audition for a permit from the city and perform in designated zones like Ximending and the Xinyi shopping district. A quiet tea mountain is not one of those zones, which makes a turntable up here pure guerrilla work — done briefly, kept low, and packed away the moment it's in anyone's way.

### What gear did you use?

A Numark PT01 Scratch — a battery-powered portable turntable built for scratch DJs — with a portable fader and a scratch record. Because it runs on batteries and fits in a shoulder bag, it can set up on a wall halfway up a mountain, which is the only reason a session on a Maokong hillside is even possible.
