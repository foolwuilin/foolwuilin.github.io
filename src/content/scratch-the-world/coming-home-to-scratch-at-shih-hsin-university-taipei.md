On 7 October 2017 I carried a battery-powered Numark PT01 Scratch back to Shih Hsin University (世新大學) — the hillside campus in Taipei's Wenshan District that has quietly meant more to me than almost any other stop on this map — and cut a few bars on ground that helped shape how I hear music.

The caption I posted that day was short and honest: "The college that has a significant meaning to me. 🃏" I tagged it 世新熱舞 and 世新街音 — the school's street-dance and street-music crews — because those are the people this place is stitched to in my memory. Coming back with a turntable in a shoulder bag felt less like busking and more like leaving a small offering on home ground.

## The campus that raised the sound

Shih Hsin isn't a hushed, buttoned-up academic institution. It's Taiwan's foremost university for journalism, broadcasting and mass communications, and that reputation soaks into everything: the student body is loud in the best way, forever expressive, always mid-project. Walk the paths and you pass people shouldering tripods, blocking out short films, chasing clean audio for a broadcast assignment. Layered under all that media energy is a genuinely respected street-dance and hip-hop culture — crews rehearse in the mirrored studios and out on the open plazas well into the evening. Setting a portable deck down in the middle of it doesn't make you the odd one out; it makes you one more part of the creative background hum the campus runs on.

## A campus cut into the mountain

The setting is half the story. Shih Hsin sits in Wenshan, a southern Taipei district of green ridgelines, old tea slopes above Muzha, and a pace that drops a gear the moment you leave the downtown grid. The campus is famously — and steeply — built straight into the flank of Xianjiyan (仙跡岩), a forested little mountain. You feel the gradient in your knees before you feel it anywhere else.

I came in the usual way: the MRT Green Line down to Jingmei Station (景美站), then a ten-to-fifteen-minute walk along Jingmei Street onto Muzha Road, over the Jingmei River, with the streetscape getting greener and more folded the closer you get. Then the signature moment — the Tunnel Entrance (山洞口), a short illuminated concrete passage you literally walk through to reach the main campus. Step out the far end and the terraces and long staircases climb away from you into the trees. It's the least generic university entrance I know, and a strangely perfect threshold to carry a record crate through.

## October light on the Wenshan hills

October is the kindest month to do this outdoors in Taipei. By early autumn the sweltering, sticky heat of summer has usually broken, and you're left with easy temperatures around 24–27°C and humidity that finally lets go of your shirt. Golden hour pours across the Wenshan hills and catches in the canopy — the campus is shaded by big banyan and camphor trees, and in mid-semester the whole place is fully alive with students back for the term.

The soundscape is what I remember most. Wind moving through the hillside trees, the campus radio station piping over the outdoor speakers, the heavy bass of a dance crew's boombox rolling out near the cafeteria, media students arguing happily over a script. A crossfader's sharp, rhythmic click doesn't fight any of that — it just slots in, one more rhythmic texture in an audio landscape that was already busy before I arrived.

## Finding a spot without getting in the way

Taiwanese university campuses work more or less like public parks: locals wander in to walk, stretch and pass the afternoon, and nobody's standing at the gate to interrogate a visitor who finds a quiet bench. That openness is what makes a session like this possible in the first place. The trick is simply to read the room.

A self-contained seven-inch rig — the little PT01 and a short stack of 45s — is built for a place this vertical. Total mobility matters when the whole campus is terraces and stairs, so I stayed light and mobile rather than planting a heavy setup anywhere. The unwritten rule is to keep clear of active lecture halls and the library, and to gravitate instead toward the open wooden decking, the benches near the student cafeteria, or the lower plazas by the tunnel where the dance crews already gather. Volume stayed modest, no tip bag, cables tucked out of the walkway. The culture here is communal but polite, and the honest move is to add to the atmosphere, not bulldoze it.

## Why Shih Hsin earns a pin on the map

Some stops on this map are landmarks I scratched at because the view demanded it. Shih Hsin is different — it earns its pin on meaning, not scenery. This is a campus tied up with the crews and the community that pulled me into this music in the first place, and coming back with the same little battery deck I've carried everywhere else felt like closing a small loop. The cuts weren't for an audience. They were a way of saying: this is where a lot of it started, and I still know the way in through the tunnel.

## Frequently asked questions

### Where was this filmed?

At Shih Hsin University (世新大學) in Taipei's Wenshan District — the hillside campus built into Xianjiyan (仙跡岩), reached from Jingmei MRT Station (景美站) on the Green Line via a walk over the Jingmei River and up Muzha Road, through the campus's distinctive illuminated Tunnel Entrance (山洞口).

### Can you just walk onto a Taiwanese university campus like this?

Largely, yes. Public university campuses in Taiwan function much like open public parks — locals routinely walk in to exercise, stroll or hang out, and there are no strict gatekeepers stopping a visitor who wants to sit on a bench. For a tiny, quiet, battery-powered setup with no tip jar, that reads as personal practice. The courteous approach is to keep away from lecture halls and the library, keep the volume low, and move on cheerfully if anyone asks.

### Why does Shih Hsin University mean something to you?

Because of its people more than its buildings. Shih Hsin has a deep street-dance and street-music culture — the 世新熱舞 and 世新街音 crowds I tagged in the caption — and that community is bound up with how I came into this music. Bringing a turntable back there was a personal homecoming, not just another backdrop.

### What gear did you use?

A battery-powered Numark PT01 Scratch — the same little portable deck I've carried through this whole series, chosen because it runs on batteries and fits in a bag, so any step, plaza edge or stretch of wooden decking can become a booth.
