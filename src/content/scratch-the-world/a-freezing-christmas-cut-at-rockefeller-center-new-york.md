Rockefeller Center is one of those places you have already seen a thousand times before you ever stand in it — the tree, the sunken rink, the gold statue, the crowds. The day after Christmas in 2017 I finally set a little portable turntable down in the middle of all that, in a record-breaking cold snap, and cut a few bars in the freezing Midtown air. I'm DJ FOOL Wuilin (吳奕霖), and this stop on Scratch the World was short, brutally cold, and completely worth it. Here's what it's really like to scratch at Rockefeller Center in the dead of a New York winter.

## The day after Christmas, in a record cold snap

I posted that clip with "before the day is gone, Merry Merry Christmas" still on my mind, because it was December 26 and the city had barely come down from the holiday. What I hadn't planned for was the weather. Late December 2017 brought one of the coldest end-of-year stretches New York had seen in years — an Arctic blast that rolled in right after Christmas Day and stayed. Daytime highs sat in the teens and low twenties Fahrenheit, and the wind chill pushed below zero. You could see your own breath hanging in front of the turntable. That cold becomes part of the story the moment you unzip the bag.

## Rockefeller Center in the blue hour

If you have only seen Rockefeller Center on television, the first surprise is how compact and vertical it feels in person. It's a twenty-two-acre run of Art Deco limestone between Fifth and Sixth Avenues, stretching from the high 40s up to 51st Street — a whole "city within a city" of bronze reliefs and setback towers. The Channel Gardens slope down from Fifth Avenue toward the plaza, and in late December the promenade is lined with the lit wire-frame angels blowing their long trumpets. At the bottom, the rink is a sunken rectangle that looks enormous on screen and is honestly tiny in person, with the gold-leaf Prometheus hanging over the ice and the tree towering above it, something like fifty thousand lights on one tree.

By about 4:30 in the afternoon the winter sky drops into a deep blue-black, and that's when the place turns electric: the tree, the gold statue, the glowing Saks facade across Fifth Avenue, and a river of yellow cab headlights all reflecting off wet, salted pavement. Scratching into that light, with your breath fogging up between you and the faders, is a feeling I hadn't had anywhere else on the map.

## Where you can actually set up

Here's the practical truth most tourists never notice: the plaza itself, Rockefeller Plaza, is private property — it's owned and run by Tishman Speyer, not the city — and during the holiday crush private security will not let you unpack gear and busk on it. So you do what every street performer in Midtown does and work the public sidewalks just outside the property line. The reliable spots are Fifth Avenue up by 49th and 50th, in front of St. Patrick's Cathedral and Saks; Sixth Avenue outside Radio City Music Hall; and, when the wind is genuinely dangerous, the 47–50th Street subway mezzanine downstairs, where the air is still and warm and the foot traffic never stops.

On the legal side, New York is more generous than people expect. Unamplified street performance on a public sidewalk is legal without any permit, as long as you aren't blocking a doorway or the flow of people. The moment you add a battery speaker you technically need an NYPD Sound Device Permit — about forty-five dollars a day, applied for in person at the local precinct (Midtown North, on West 54th). In reality, nobody pulling a two-minute portable session is filing paperwork; what actually matters is your footprint. Clog the Fifth Avenue bottleneck or spill your little crowd into the crosswalk during the holidays and either NYPD or Rockefeller security will have you packed up and moving in seconds.

## The rig, and what the cold does to it

My setup was the same one I carry everywhere: a Numark PT01 Scratch and a wireless Mixfader. What the cold does to that rig is its own lesson. The slipmat drags noticeably thicker when it's below freezing, so the record fights you a little. Your fingertips go numb within a few minutes, which blunts your touch on the fader exactly when you need it most. The lithium batteries drain fast in the cold, so a charge that lasts all afternoon in summer gives you a fraction of that here. The honest move is to keep the session short: get the rig open, cut hard for a couple of minutes while your hands still work, grab the clip, and get everything back in the bag before the gear — or you — stops cooperating.

## What it felt like out there

The soundscape is the part I keep coming back to. Midtown in that week is a canyon of noise: NYPD whistles chirping at the intersections, skate blades carving the ice down in the rink, vendors calling out "Hot nuts! Pretzels!", the distant bass of the light show on the Saks building across the avenue, and underneath all of it the muffled rush of thousands of padded coats sliding past each other. The air smells like roasted cinnamon-sugar nuts off the stainless pushcarts mixed with bus diesel and cold wool. And the tourists — moving in those slow holiday waves — are weirdly the best audience you can ask for, because a compact turntable with a fader is so unexpected next to the usual brass bands that people do a genuine double-take and stop. For a couple of freezing minutes, in the biggest, most-photographed plaza in the world, a little portable deck held its own.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is portablism?

Portablism is scratching with a small, portable turntable instead of full-size decks. It shrinks a whole DJ setup down to a little turntable and a fader you can carry in a bag — which is exactly why you can open up on a Manhattan sidewalk in the middle of winter and start cutting.

### Where can you perform near Rockefeller Center?

Not on the plaza itself, which is private. Street performers work the public sidewalks around it: Fifth Avenue by St. Patrick's Cathedral and Saks, Sixth Avenue outside Radio City Music Hall, and — when it's bitterly cold — the 47–50th Street subway mezzanine, which is sheltered from the wind and full of foot traffic.

### Do you need a permit to scratch on the street in New York?

For unamplified playing on a public sidewalk, no — it's legal as long as you don't block pedestrians or doorways. Once you add any battery-powered amplification you technically need an NYPD Sound Device Permit (around $45 a day, obtained in person at the local precinct). Either way, the thing that actually keeps you out of trouble is not blocking the flow of people.

### How do you scratch outdoors in freezing weather?

Keep it short and protect your hands and batteries. Below freezing, your slipmat drags harder, your fingertips go numb fast, and lithium batteries drain quickly, so you set up, cut for a couple of focused minutes, capture the take, and pack down before the cold wins.
