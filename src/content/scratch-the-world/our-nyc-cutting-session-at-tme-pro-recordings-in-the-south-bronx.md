**Quick answer:** On November 13, 2017, I swapped the cold New York streets for a warm room: a NYC cutting session at T.M.E. PRO Recordings, a working hip-hop studio at the corner of Lincoln Avenue and Bruckner Boulevard in the South Bronx. One table of turntables, the Skratcher New Jersey and New York crew, and everyone trading cuts over a single beat. That is the whole video.

## Come join us: why this session moved indoors

The caption I posted that night was an open invitation: *come to join us, NYC cutting session.* After my [freezing afternoon in Central Park](/scratch-the-world/scratching-in-central-park-during-a-november-cold-snap/) the day before, the thought of scratching outside again did not appeal. Mid-November is exactly when the New York portablist crews move indoors, and that week the invitation came from the South Bronx. A cutting session is not a gig and not a battle. It is a jam: someone runs a loop, the turntables get chained into one mixer, and the DJs trade four or eight bars each, passing the beat around the table the way jazz players trade fours. I had carried my Numark PT01 Scratch around Manhattan for months; this was the first time it sat on a table with so many other decks.

## Where T.M.E. PRO Recordings is, and why the Bronx matters

T.M.E. PRO Recordings sits at 127 Lincoln Avenue, right where Lincoln meets Bruckner Boulevard in Mott Haven, the southern tip of the Bronx. The studio is run by Fred Ones, a South Bronx producer and engineer who came up through DJ and graffiti culture, and it has been a working room for underground New York hip-hop for years. The building is a three-story pre-war brick loft from 1931, with wooden floors and tall windows looking down on Bruckner, and by 2017 the ground floor was home to Bronx Native, a local apparel shop that puts Bronx pride on a T-shirt.

For a scratch DJ this zip code is holy ground. Hip-hop was born a few miles up the Harlem River at 1520 Sedgwick Avenue in 1973, and the scratch itself was pioneered in the Bronx by Grand Wizzard Theodore. Cutting records inside a Bronx studio run by a Bronx DJ felt like plugging straight into the source.

## Getting there on a November evening

From Manhattan I rode the uptown 6 train along Lexington Avenue. After 125th Street the train dips under the Harlem River and comes up at 3 Av–138 St, the first stop in the Bronx. From there it is a short walk south on Lincoln Avenue to Bruckner Boulevard. By five o'clock the sun was already gone behind the Manhattan skyline, and a damp wind came off the river between the Third Avenue Bridge one block west and the Willis Avenue Bridge a couple of blocks east. Overhead, the Major Deegan and the Bruckner Expressway rumble with trucks, and the old factory lofts still carry faded ghost signs from the days when this was the Bronx Piano District, with the Estey Piano factory and its clock tower standing just down Lincoln Avenue. Bruckner Boulevard between Lincoln and Alexander Avenues was once marketed as an antiques row, and in 2017 it was somewhere in between: a few vintage storefronts, graffiti-tagged roll-down gates, and Bruckner Bar & Grill on the corner.

## Inside the room: posters, radiators and a table of turntables

Inside, the first thing I felt was the heat of the steam radiators after the river wind, and the first thing I saw was a wall of hip-hop posters and record sleeves, which you can spot behind us in the video. The turntables took over a table in the middle of the room: full-size decks with a mixer between them, plus a line of portables, mine among them. You could feel the low hum of Bruckner Boulevard traffic through the floor, and once the loop came on the room filled with the click of faders and the chirps and flares cutting across the beat. A friend in a gold Skratcher New Jersey tee took his turn while the rest of us nodded, snapped fingers and waited for the downbeat to drop in. There is a kind of listening you only do when you are up next.

## Cutting-session etiquette I learned that night

A few unwritten rules kept the session smooth, and I now carry them into every studio I visit:

- Show up self-contained: your own 3.5 mm-to-RCA cable, a charged battery, and a splitter in case the mixer channels are full.
- Set your turntable's output to around 70 to 80 percent before you plug in, and turn it down before you pull a cable. Studio monitors are expensive.
- Keep to four or eight bars and hand off cleanly on the downbeat. Hogging the loop kills the room.
- Keep coffee and drinks off the gear table, and never touch the studio console without the host's nod.
- Bring stickers or a 7-inch from home to trade. It is the fastest way to make friends in a room like this.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a cutting session?

A cutting session is a jam for scratch DJs. A beat loops over the speakers, the turntables are connected to one mixer, and each DJ takes a turn of four or eight bars before passing to the next. It is a session for swapping ideas and sharpening timing, not a competition.

### Where is T.M.E. PRO Recordings?

It is at 127 Lincoln Avenue at Bruckner Boulevard in Mott Haven, in the South Bronx, New York. The closest subway stop is 3 Av–138 St on the 6 train, a few blocks north.

### Can a visitor join a NYC portablist cutting session?

Usually yes, if you ask first. Sessions are often announced by crews like Skratcher New Jersey and New York, and visitors with their own portable turntable are welcome as long as they bring their own cables and respect the host's space and the turn order.

### What gear did you use?

My usual rig: a Numark PT01 Scratch portable turntable with a Mixfader for the crossfader work, plugged into the studio mixer alongside everyone else's portables.
