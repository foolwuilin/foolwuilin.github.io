On 27 September 2017 I went back to a place I used to haunt — the wide granite plaza and covered arcades of Taipei's National Dr. Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hall (國立國父紀念館) — set my battery-powered Numark PT01 down on the stone, and cut a few bars where I'd spent so many afternoons growing up. The caption was just a few words: "the place I used to be hanging out." Of all the pins on the Scratch the World map, this is the one closest to home.

Some stops on this journey are about landing somewhere brand new. This one was the opposite — a return. Long before I was carrying a portable turntable around the world, I was a kid in Taipei hanging out under these colonnades, and coming back with a PT01 instead of dance shoes felt like closing a loop I didn't know was open.

## Coming up out of Exit 4

If you want to find this place, take the MRT Blue (Bannan) Line to Sun Yat-Sen Memorial Hall Station and walk up Exit 4 — you surface right at the northern gateway of the grounds. The superblock sits between Zhongxiao East Road to the north and Ren'ai Road to the south, a green island of banyan trees in the middle of the city. Ahead of you the hall itself rises on its podium: architect Wang Da-hong's 1972 design, a sweeping upturned roof of golden-glazed tile that reads like an imperial palace redrawn in clean, mid-century lines, floating over a ring of sturdy colonnades. Look southeast across the open plaza and Taipei 101 stands there in a clean, unobstructed line — the whole square framed against the towers of Xinyi. I've walked out of that exit hundreds of times, and it still lands the same way.

## The place I used to be hanging out

Here's the thing tourists don't always clock: the wide covered arcades flanking the main hall are the unofficial headquarters of Taipei's whole street-dance scene. The deep roof overhangs keep off both the subtropical downpours and the brutal midday sun, and the floor is laid in seamless polished terrazzo and granite — slick enough for headspins and gliding, firm enough for popping and locking. Best of all, the hall's big tinted ground-level windows work as full-length mirrors, so high-school clubs, university crews and veteran b-boys line up and drill choreography in their own reflection. Any weekday afternoon you'll find a dozen crews clustered around portable Bluetooth speakers, counting out eights over funk and hip-hop breaks, the whole colonnade squeaking with the sound of sneakers on stone. This is where I grew up in the culture. Coming back to cut here, with a turntable under my hands instead of a spot in the cypher, felt like paying the place back.

## Setting down the PT01

The beauty of the Numark PT01 Scratch is that it doesn't need anything — no wall, no mixer, no stage. Battery-powered, it just goes wherever I set it down, and that afternoon I set it down on the granite at the edge of the arcades, right where the dancers were running their routines. I kept the volume low and local, the way you do here, and started cutting a few bars into the gap between their eight-counts. Nobody blinks at a small setup like that in this space; a portable turntable ticking along at conversation volume folds straight into the mix of speakers and sneakers. For a few minutes it was just me, the stone, the mirrors, and the muscle memory of a place I knew by heart.

## What the plaza feels like

The plaza changes character by the hour. In the early morning it belongs to the tai-chi and fan-dance groups, soft counting voices drifting across the stone. By the middle of the afternoon the sun radiates straight up off the unshaded granite and pushes everyone into the shade of the walkways, the glare lighting up the terracotta-gold of the roof against a hard blue sky. Drift over to the southwest corner and you hit Cui Lake — Emerald Lake — with its arched stone footbridges, willows leaning over carp-filled water, and older folks parked at the pavilions playing chess. On the hour, inside the hall's soaring atrium, the honor guard performs the changing of the guard beneath the 5.8-metre bronze statue of Sun Yat-sen, rifles spinning and hobnailed boots stamping the marble in unison. Then evening comes, the ground uplights warm up, and people settle onto the wide granite steps with cold drinks and milk tea. I've seen this place in every one of those moods.

## Why this pin matters

Every other stop on this map is me showing up somewhere as a visitor and leaving a scratch behind. This one is the reverse: a scratch left in the exact spot that made me. Manhattan is where Scratch the World started, but Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hall is where *I* started — the arcades where I learned to move, the mirror I grew up watching, the floor a thousand crews have worn smooth. Bringing the turntable home and cutting a few bars there was my way of saying thanks to the place. If you've got a spot like that, go back to it with whatever you make now. It hits different.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is portablism?

Portablism is scratching on a small, portable turntable instead of full-size decks. It shrinks a DJ rig down to a battery-powered turntable and a fader you can carry in a bag — which is exactly why you can set up on a park plaza, a colonnade, or a street corner and start cutting without a wall or a mixer in sight.

### How do I get to Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hall?

Take the Taipei MRT Blue (Bannan) Line to Sun Yat-Sen Memorial Hall Station and leave by Exit 4, which comes up at the northern edge of the grounds. It's in the middle of the city between Zhongxiao East Road and Ren'ai Road, an easy walk from Taipei 101 and the Xinyi shopping district.

### Why do dancers gather at Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hall?

The covered arcades around the hall are made for it, almost by accident: deep roofs that block rain and sun, a smooth polished terrazzo-and-granite floor that's perfect for footwork, and huge tinted windows that act as full-length mirrors. Generations of Taipei crews have practiced there, which is how it became the city's unofficial street-dance headquarters.

### Can you play music or perform at the plaza?

Informally, yes — buskers, acoustic players and dance crews are a normal part of the scene, mostly around the arcades and the open stones. The unwritten rule is to keep it low-key: a battery-powered speaker or portable turntable at conversation volume blends right in, while loud amplification that carries into the halls tends to draw a word from roving park security. Keep the sound local to your own little circle and you're part of the furniture.
