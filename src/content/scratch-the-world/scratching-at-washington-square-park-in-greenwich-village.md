On 4 November 2017 I set a battery-powered Numark PT01 Scratch down in Washington Square Park, in the middle of Greenwich Village, and cut for a while in the shadow of the big marble arch. My caption that day was simply “Here’s also a good spot for street activities.” — and after a few autumn evenings in Manhattan parks, I’d say Washington Square is the best-known stage of them all.

## A park built on a potter’s field

Washington Square Park is a 9.75-acre public square at the bottom of Fifth Avenue, and it carries a lot of history under its paving stones: it was a 19th-century potter’s field, and thousands of burials are still said to lie beneath it. Today the first thing you see is the Washington Square Arch, a 77-foot Tuckahoe marble monument designed by Stanford White and finished in 1892 to honour George Washington. South of the arch is the central fountain plaza, and in early November the fountain is usually drained for the season, which turns the sunken basin and its wide stone rim into a natural amphitheatre. It’s the park’s main performance ring, and you feel that the moment you walk in.

## Streets, students and a bohemian corridor

The square is boxed in by very different pieces of New York. To the north, Washington Square North is lined with “The Row”, a run of 1830s Greek Revival townhouses that look like old-money Manhattan. To the south and east, New York University practically swallows the park; the big red-sandstone Bobst Library looms over the south side, so the paths are always full of students with coffees and backpacks. A couple of blocks west, MacDougal Street and Bleecker Street form the old bohemian corridor of comedy clubs, basement jazz rooms, falafel stands and historic cafés such as Caffe Reggio. If you carry a portable turntable, that mix of students, tourists and locals is a perfect audience: plenty of people with a few minutes to spare, and nobody in a rush.

## What early November felt like

Early November is the tail end of autumn, and it’s lovely. The London plane and ginkgo trees drop thick yellow and brown leaves across the asphalt, and by about half past three the sun is low enough to throw a sharp golden glare on the arch. The air is brisk enough that you can see your breath. It smells of roasted honey peanuts from the carts around the edge of the park, a little exhaust drifting across from Sixth Avenue, and the unmistakable smell of weed that has always been part of the park.

The noise floor is high, and I love that about the place. There are sirens, skateboard wheels clacking over the joints in the paving, and layers of live music coming from every direction. Scratching in there is less about hearing yourself perfectly and more about finding the gaps in the city’s own rhythm.

## How a scratch DJ fits in with the park’s performers

Washington Square already runs on an unspoken pecking order of street performers. The fountain basin is the centre of the action, where acrobatic b-boy crews gather big, shoulder-to-shoulder crowds and need plenty of room to tumble. Along the pathways you’ll hear solo musicians, and the park is famous for the pianist who rolls a baby grand piano out onto the paths. A tiny PT01 on a bench or ledge sits at the quiet end of all that: no stage, no big speakers, just a turntable, a mixfader and a few cuts. I treat it as joining the park, not taking it over — I stay out of the middle, stay clear of anyone who’s already drawing a crowd, and keep to the edges where people can wander over if they’re curious.

## Etiquette for playing a portable turntable in the park

New York can require permits for amplified sound, and enforcement mostly targets big, generator-powered PA systems. A single DJ on a small battery rig at modest volume is a very different thing, and in my experience it tends to be tolerated, but you should always be ready to turn it down or move if someone asks. The key rule is space: if a few people stop to watch, keep them out of the walking lanes, because blocking foot traffic is the quickest way to get a tap on the shoulder from the police. Be friendly to the regulars, and don’t set up on top of another performer’s pitch. Finally, plan for the cold: wind funnels through the arch, your slipmat wants a firm hand, and your fingers stiffen after about half an hour of cutting.

## Frequently asked questions

### Where did you scratch on 4 November 2017?

In Washington Square Park, in Greenwich Village, Manhattan. The park sits at the southern end of Fifth Avenue, just north of the NYU buildings and a short walk from MacDougal and Bleecker Streets.

### Do you need a permit to scratch in Washington Square Park?

Amplified sound can need a permit in New York City, and larger sound systems are what usually get moved along. I keep everything small, battery-powered and at conversational volume, and I always pack up politely if asked. Check the current NYC Parks rules before you go.

### What is the best time of day to play in the park?

Late afternoon in autumn is hard to beat: the crowds are thick, the light is golden on the arch, and it isn’t yet freezing. Weekends are busiest, so give the bigger acts their space.

### What gear did you use?

A Numark PT01 Scratch, a portable battery-powered turntable with a built-in mixfader. It’s small enough to carry in a bag and set down on a bench or low wall.
