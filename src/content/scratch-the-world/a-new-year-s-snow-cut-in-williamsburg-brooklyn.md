I started 2018 the way I had ended 2017: cold, stubborn, and looking for somewhere to set a portable turntable down in the snow. Two nights earlier I had been in the middle of the Times Square countdown; on the first morning of the year I took the train back out to Williamsburg, Brooklyn, where a few weeks of winter had already taught me that snow and a battery deck are a complicated friendship. My caption that day was just three words and a war cry — "Round 2, FIGHT!!!!" — because I knew exactly what I was walking into.

## Round two on the snow

This was not my first snow cut in Williamsburg, and that mattered. I had already stood in the slush here a few weeks before, and I had already watched the cold do strange things to my gear. Coming back on New Year's Day was half stubbornness and half experiment: I wanted to know whether the problem was the weather or the equipment. "After two times cutting on the snow," I wrote under the clip, "I found that the Innofader always went crazy." So round two was less about chasing a crowd and more about proving to myself that I could still land a clean cut with the whole river trying to freeze my hands off.

## A New Year's Day hush over Bedford Avenue

Williamsburg on the first morning of the year is about as quiet as this neighbourhood ever gets. The delivery e-bikes that usually swarm Bedford Avenue were almost gone; the foot traffic had thinned to bundled-up dog walkers, a couple of runners making slow loops of the track in McCarren Park, and a few hungover locals moving carefully between corner cafés with both hands wrapped around their coffee. The fresh snow pulled a muffled hush over everything — the particular quiet you only get when powder has swallowed the street noise — and then every few minutes the J, M and Z trains would come clattering across the Williamsburg Bridge with that long metallic rumble and punch a clean hole straight through it. I waited until the middle of the day to set up; there is an unspoken etiquette to letting a neighbourhood sleep off New Year's Eve before you start throwing sharp fader cuts off the brick.

## The river, the ice, and the skyline

I walked down toward the water to look before I played. The East River on a January morning is a hard, beautiful thing — slate-grey and choppy, with small chunks of ice drifting past on the tidal current, and the whole vertical wall of Manhattan standing up sharp and high-contrast on the far bank: the Empire State, the Chrysler, One World Trade all cut clean against a pale winter sky. Behind me the teal gantry cranes of the old Domino Sugar Refinery loomed over the waterfront, eighty feet of rusting industrial steel the city had not yet finished turning into a park. The wind came straight off the river and funnelled down the numbered cross streets like a tunnel, carrying a sharp, wet cold that found every gap in my gloves within seconds. It is a gorgeous place to stand with a turntable. It is a brutal place to actually use one.

## When the fader froze

Here is the part I actually came to test. The first time I cut on the snow, and again the second, my Innofader had simply quit on me — after about ten minutes out in the cold and the damp it stopped cutting altogether, no sharp click at the fader, no clean on and off, nothing. I am fairly sure the cold and the moisture were working their way into it; a crossfader lives or dies on a crisp cut, and mine had gone soft and useless right when I needed it most. So on New Year's Day I swapped it out for my Jesse Dean JDDX2R, and — fortunately — that one kept working. That is the whole hidden story of winter portablism: the deck is the easy part, and the fader is the thing that betrays you. Lithium batteries drain fast in the cold, belt-drive platters stiffen until they warm up, and condensation fogs everything the moment you carry it back indoors — but on that day it was the fader that decided whether I had a show at all. I keep the spare parts warm in an inside pocket now because of exactly this morning.

## A crowd that knows a break when it hears one

Williamsburg is the right place to lose a fader and keep going, because the people here actually know what they are listening to. This is a neighbourhood built on record shops and DIY lofts — Earwax Records a few blocks over, a long memory of warehouse parties, b-boy cyphers and brass bands under the bridge at Continental Army Plaza. Nobody here treats a turntable on the sidewalk as a novelty. In the cold, people walk with their collars up and their minds made up, but when I got a real boom-bap break juggling under my fingers, a few of them slowed down: a nod, a phone lifted for a short clip, a couple of bills folded into the open record bag. On the first day of a new year, in falling snow, catching even three strangers with an honest scratch felt like exactly the right way to start 2018.

## Frequently asked questions

### Where did you scratch on New Year's Day 2018?

In Williamsburg, Brooklyn — the same North Williamsburg streets around Bedford Avenue and the East River waterfront where I had cut in the December snow, with the old Domino Sugar Refinery cranes standing over the river and the steel of the Williamsburg Bridge humming away to the south.

### Why did your crossfader stop working in the cold?

My Innofader kept dying after about ten minutes outdoors — I am fairly sure the cold and the wet were getting into it, because the cut just went dead, with no clean on and off at all. Crossfaders are precision parts, and freezing, damp air is hard on them. On New Year's Day I switched to a Jesse Dean JDDX2R, which held up fine, and these days I keep the gear and the spare batteries warm in an inside coat pocket right up until the moment I play.

### How do you keep scratching with numb hands in the snow?

You stop fighting the cold and work with it. I wear thin liner or fingerless gloves so I can still feel the record edge, tuck hand-warmer packets into my pockets between routines, and lean away from fast flares and crabs toward heavier tears and slow baby scratches — the cuts you can still land when you cannot feel your fingertips.

### What gear did you use?

A Numark PT01 Scratch — a battery-powered portable turntable — this time paired with a Jesse Dean JDDX2R crossfader after the Innofader gave out in the cold. The whole rig fits in a shoulder bag and sets up on a ledge in under a minute, which is the only reason taking it out into a Brooklyn winter is even thinkable.
