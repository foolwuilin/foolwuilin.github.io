On 3 September 2017 I set my battery-powered Numark PT01 down on the wide stone plaza in front of the National Dr. Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hall (國立國父紀念館) in Taipei and cut a few bars for the dancers who were already drilling their routines there. Two days after a guerrilla session inside a gadget mall, this was the opposite kind of stage — a vast, open civic plaza that quietly turns into the city's street-dance studio every evening. Here's what it's like to scratch a portable turntable on Taipei's front porch, with a caption that just read "Dancer's delight lol."

## Out of Exit 4 and onto Taipei's front porch

I came up out of Exit 4 of Sun Yat-Sen Memorial Hall MRT Station and the space just opens up in front of you. This is the upscale, humming edge of Xinyi District, hemmed in by the wide, leafy lanes of Renai Road and Guangfu South Road, with Taipei 101 towering just a few blocks east like a giant glass bamboo shoot. Then there's the hall itself: a colossal monument finished in 1972 to honour the founding father of the Republic of China, designed by the architect Wang Da-hong as a striking blend of modernist concrete pillars and a sweeping, traditional yellow-tiled Chinese roof. It sits inside the 115,000-square-metre grounds of Zhongshan Park, and the whole thing is anchored by a massive, flat front plaza paved in smooth grey stone tiles — a vast expanse of open ground that really does act as the city's front porch. After a cramped gadget mall, all that sky felt like exhaling.

## The plaza that dances every evening

The reason I came here is the same reason the caption was so short. The hall's dark, tinted glass doors and its long shaded corridors act like giant impromptu mirrors, and that has quietly turned the perimeter of this solemn national monument into Taipei's de facto ground zero for urban street culture. Every evening, breaking crews, poppers, hip-hop choreographers and skaters claim their own patches of the smooth tiles and drill routines for hours, each group orbiting its own Bluetooth speaker. Walking a battery-powered turntable into the middle of that felt less like crashing the party and more like joining it — "Dancer's delight lol," because I wasn't the show, the dancers were, and a DJ cutting vinyl is really just an analog heartbeat under all that visual rhythm.

## Setting up on the warm stone

By late September Taipei's brutal summer heat had finally started to break, but those pale stone tiles still radiate the afternoon sun back up at you long after you'd expect them to cool. I'd timed it for golden hour, which is genuinely beautiful here: the low sun bounces off the surrounding glass skyscrapers and throws long, geometric shadows off the hall's massive concrete pillars, and as dusk settles the eastern skyline lights up with the glowing tiers of Taipei 101. I balanced the PT01 on a low ledge at the edge of the plaza, dropped the needle, and let my cuts fold into the layered soundscape already going — the distant hum of scooters pouring down Guangfu South Road, the rhythmic squeak of rubber soles on stone, the overlapping bass from a dozen speakers, and my own sharp, percussive chika-chika of a crossfader chopping a sample.

## Scratching in the rules' blind spot

None of this was strictly by the book, and that's worth being honest about. Since the early 2000s, street performance in Taiwan has been a licensed activity — buskers (街頭藝人) audition for a permit from the city's Department of Cultural Affairs and are meant to stick to designated zones — so setting up a PA and a tip jar to busk for money without that licence can earn you a swift fine. Simply setting up a portable rig like a PT01 to practise and jam, with no hat out for cash, generally slips under that line as part of the plaza's rehearsal culture. The etiquette that really matters here, though, is about reverence: inside the hall there's a solemn changing of the honour guard at the statue of Dr. Sun Yat-sen every hour, and the main entrance steps and central ceremonial pathways are no place for a turntable. Stick to the flanking corridors and the outer edges of the plaza, keep your volume balanced so it doesn't drown out a neighbouring dance crew, and if a security guard drifts over, the respected way out is a quick bow, a soft "bù hǎoyìsi" (excuse me), and packing up smoothly.

## Why the memorial plaza was the perfect stop

Scratch the World has always been about carrying a turntable into ordinary, unexpected, in-between places and leaving a little sound behind. A grand national monument that also happens to double, every single evening, as an open-air teenage dance studio is about the most fitting stage I could ask for — solemn stone and youthful noise sharing the exact same tiles. Everything I needed still fit in one bag slung over a shoulder, and that portability is the whole point of the project. Two days in, Taiwan's run was deepening from a novelty stop into something that felt like home ground, and the map was only getting richer.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can you busk or DJ at Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hall?

Not formally without a licence. Street performance in Taiwan is regulated — buskers audition for a permit from the Department of Cultural Affairs and perform in designated public zones — so busking with a PA and a tip jar for money can draw a fine. In practice, quietly setting up a portable rig to practise or jam, without soliciting money, is broadly tolerated on the plaza as part of its rehearsal culture. Just keep well clear of the ceremonial steps and the hourly honour-guard area, and keep your volume respectful.

### What is the National Dr. Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hall?

It's a colossal monument in Taipei's Xinyi District, completed in 1972 to honour Dr. Sun Yat-sen, the founding father of the Republic of China. Designed by architect Wang Da-hong, it pairs modernist concrete pillars with a sweeping yellow-tiled roof, and sits inside the 115,000-square-metre Zhongshan Park, just a few blocks from Taipei 101 and beside its own MRT station. Inside, an honour guard performs a changing ceremony at Dr. Sun's statue every hour.

### Why is the plaza always full of dancers?

The building's big tinted-glass doors and shaded corridors work as giant mirrors, and the flat, smooth stone tiles are ideal to dance on, so the plaza has become Taipei's unofficial home for breaking, popping and street dance. On most evenings you'll find multiple crews rehearsing side by side, each with their own speaker — which is exactly the crowd a portable scratch set slots into.

### What gear did you use?

A Numark PT01 Scratch — a battery-powered portable turntable built for scratch DJs — with a portable fader and a 7-inch scratch record. Because it runs on batteries and fits in a shoulder bag, it sets up anywhere, which is the only reason a session on a Taipei plaza is even possible.
