On a freezing December evening in 2017 I finally admitted defeat against the New York street and carried a battery-powered Numark PT01 Scratch indoors — into the glowing Winter Garden atrium at Brookfield Place, under 45-foot palms and a whole ceiling of holiday lanterns. Here is what it is like to scratch a portable turntable inside Lower Manhattan's warmest, shiniest marble cathedral, quietly and completely off the books.

My caption that night was half a joke and half a confession: "Maybe cutting indoors is a good idea. It's warm in here, and there are so many fancy lights." After a run of sessions spent cutting with numb fingers on frozen plazas, the idea of a heated room with a light show overhead felt almost like cheating.

## Giving up on the street

I had come down to Battery Park City meaning to play outside, by the water. But in mid-December the Hudson does not negotiate: twenty-knot wind pours straight across North Cove Marina, the mega-yachts wintering at the docks knock their halyards against aluminum masts, and the cold finds every gap in your gloves before you have even unzipped the bag. Over on the upper plaza the Rink at Brookfield Place was still turning, skaters scraping the ice, river air leaking in through the revolving doors. I lasted about as long as my hands did, then pushed through those doors — and the building hit me with an instant wall of seventy-degree heat, the smell of pine from the holiday wreaths, polished stone, and the muffled clink of wine glasses from the restaurants along the edge. That was the moment I decided the street could wait.

## A glass cathedral full of palms

The Winter Garden is the kind of room that makes you stop and look up. César Pelli designed it, it opened in 1988, and it was rebuilt after 9/11 damaged the original — a barrel-vaulted glass roof arcing up roughly 120 feet, ten stories of steel trusses and black glass that at night throws the interior light back down at you instead of showing the sky. Underneath it runs a formal double row of sixteen Washingtonia robusta palms, forty to forty-five feet tall, rising straight out of iron grates set flush into the floor — a tropical nave growing inside a glass-and-steel cathedral. To one side, a sweeping semicircle of travertine steps pours down into the floor like an amphitheater, and people sit there with coffee and shopping bags and watch the room go by. I set the PT01 down near the base of one of the palms, where a square stone planter gave me something to lean on and a little cover from the main flow of foot traffic.

## Scratching under 650 lanterns

The reason everything in my clip glows is that December 2017 was the season of Luminaries, the holiday installation by the LAB at Rockwell Group. Suspended up among the glass ribs, over the heads of the palms, hung a geometric field of 650 custom LED lanterns. Every half hour or so they ran a choreographed four- or five-minute show set to music, washing the whole atrium in slow gradients — champagne gold, then crimson, then royal purple, icy cyan, and a deep forest green that is the color you see flooding the ceiling behind me on the video. Down on the marble floor there were three "Wishing Stations" where you could touch a panel and send a ripple of color chasing up through the lanterns, each touch quietly triggering a small charitable donation. Cutting a few bars with that going on above you is a strange, lovely feeling: you are the smallest, scrappiest light source in a room engineered to glow.

## How the room actually sounds

A huge glass-and-stone atrium is a gift and a trap for a scratch DJ. The natural reverb in there is cavernous — a wet three-to-four-second decay that smears every footstep, every laugh, every rolling suitcase into one soft cathedral hum. That hum is cover, but it also eats sound. The PT01's little onboard speaker is only three inches across, and in that volume the low end simply vanishes — bass notes go thin and get swallowed whole. What survives, beautifully, is the top. A bright one-kilohertz "ahh" or "fresh" cut carries clean across the stone and hangs in the air for a second longer than it should. So I played to the room: short, high, rhythmic stabs rather than anything that needed weight behind it, letting the reverb do half the work and leaning into the handful of frequencies the space would actually let me keep.

## Playing it cool with the guards

Here is the honest part: Brookfield Place is not a subway platform and it is not a public sidewalk. There is no MTA Arts & Design program here and no First Amendment street-performer tradition — it is privately owned and professionally managed by Brookfield Properties, and unpermitted performance is simply not allowed. Security is real and visible: suited house staff plus Allied Universal guards, posted near the waterfront entrances and up by the Hudson Eats food hall on the second level, patrolling past the foot of the marble stairs. So you do not set up like a busker. No open turntable lid for tips, no case on the floor, nothing that reads as panhandling — that gets you shut down inside a minute. The trick is to look like a musician quietly testing a piece of battery gear: headphones around the neck, volume kept at half so it never travels past ten or fifteen feet, tucked against a palm planter or perched two-thirds of the way up the staircase where you pass for a seated patron fiddling with electronics. Do that and you buy yourself a real five or ten minutes before a guard wanders over with a polite, firm "Excuse me — you can't play that here." You nod, you pack up, you are already grateful you got the shot. Across West Street the Oculus and One World Trade glow white over the whole scene, Le District's bakery smell drifts down the concourse, and the contrast is the whole point: raw hip-hop gear and a clicking fader against marble, palms, and luxury storefronts.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can you busk or perform inside Brookfield Place?** Not officially. It is private property managed by Brookfield Properties, so unpermitted or amplified performance isn't permitted the way street or subway busking is. A brief, low-volume moment on a battery turntable is tolerated only until security asks you to stop — treat it as a quick guerrilla hit, not a pitch.

**Isn't a giant glass atrium too echoey to scratch in?** It is extremely live — three to four seconds of reverb. Bass disappears, but bright high-frequency scratches cut through cleanly. You adapt by playing short, high, rhythmic patterns and letting the room's reverb carry them.

**What gear did you use?** A battery-powered Numark PT01 Scratch with a wireless Mixfader crossfader — small enough to pull out of a shoulder bag, run off batteries, and pack away the second you need to move.

**When are the Winter Garden holiday lights up?** The big installations run through the December holiday season each year. In 2017 it was Luminaries, with 650 LED lanterns and timed color shows every half hour — worth checking the schedule if you want the full light wash overhead.
