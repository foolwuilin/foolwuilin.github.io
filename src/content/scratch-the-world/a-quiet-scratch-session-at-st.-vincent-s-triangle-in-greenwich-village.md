On 29 October 2017, I carried my Numark PT01 Scratch down to St. Vincent's Triangle, the little wedge of park where Seventh Avenue, Greenwich Avenue and West 12th Street meet in Greenwich Village. I sat at the edge of the plaza under the white steel canopy of the New York City AIDS Memorial and scratched for a while in the sun. My caption that day was short: "It's turning cold. Enjoy sunshine though." That pretty much sums up the afternoon.

## A memorial on a traffic triangle

St. Vincent's Triangle used to be a leftover scrap of pavement, created where the diagonal of Greenwich Avenue cuts across the Manhattan grid. Across the street stood St. Vincent's Hospital, which served the Village for more than 160 years until it closed in 2010. In the 1980s it was at the centre of the city's AIDS crisis and opened one of New York's first dedicated AIDS wards.

The memorial park was dedicated on World AIDS Day, 1 December 2016, to honour the more than 100,000 New Yorkers who died of AIDS, and the activists and caregivers who fought alongside them. When I visited in October 2017 it had been open for less than a year. It still felt new: clean stone, young trees, and a lot of people stopping to read before they walked on.

## Under the canopy

The centrepiece is the canopy, designed by the Brooklyn architects Studio a+i: a tall, angular roof built from white steel triangles, each one filled with thin louvred slats. From the ground it looks a bit like folded paper, or like an aircraft wing, depending on where you stand. You can see in the still from my video how it frames everything: the sky, the brick apartment buildings across Seventh Avenue, and the trees just starting to turn.

Under the canopy is a round granite water feature, and around it, spiralling across the paving stones, are lines from Walt Whitman's "Song of Myself", selected and arranged by the artist Jenny Holzer. You walk on poetry here. That was one of the first things I noticed, and it changed how I set up (more on that below).

## Late-October light and the sound of the place

The low autumn sun came in at an angle through the slats and threw sharp, striped triangles of shadow across the granite. As the afternoon went on, the patterns kept sliding slowly over the words on the ground. In the shade it was properly cold; in the sun it was warm enough to sit in a T-shirt, so I kept shuffling toward the light with the deck on my lap.

The sound was a mix I didn't expect. Seventh Avenue is loud, with buses, taxis and the occasional siren heading downtown, but the water in the fountain softens all of it into a kind of white noise. On top of that: dry leaves scraping across the stone, dog walkers heading along West 12th Street, bits of conversation. My little PT01 speaker sat right in the middle of it. It was never going to compete with the avenue, and that was fine.

## Scratching in the Village

Greenwich Village has a long history of music on the street, from the folk singers of the 1960s to the buskers in Washington Square Park a few blocks east, where brass bands and even a grand piano compete for a crowd. St. Vincent's Triangle is the opposite of that. It isn't a stage. It's a small, reflective corner where people stop, read, and move on.

So I played small. A few slow baby scratches and chirps, some longer phrases, lots of space in between. People walking through glanced over, a few smiled, and one or two stayed a minute to watch the record move. Nobody needed a show here. It felt more like playing quietly in someone's living room than busking.

Afterwards I walked down Greenwich Avenue past the Jefferson Market Library, with its red-brick Victorian Gothic clock tower, and on toward Christopher Street and the Stonewall Inn. It's a short walk, and doing it right after sitting at the memorial made the neighbourhood's history feel very close.

## How I'd set up at a memorial like this

This is a memorial first and a park second, so I set myself a few rules:

- **Stay off the words and the fountain.** Don't put your gear on the fountain edge or sit on top of the Whitman inscription. People come here to read it. I stayed at the edge of the plaza.
- **Keep the volume tiny.** The PT01's built-in speaker is enough for you and anyone right next to you. Amplified sound in New York City parks technically needs a permit, so small and quiet is the way to go.
- **Don't block the path.** The triangle is a shortcut for a lot of people. Keep everything in your lap or at your feet: no tables, no crates.
- **Read the room.** If someone is sitting quietly or is clearly here for personal reasons, pause or move. That matters more than finishing your routine.
- **Carry everything out.** Batteries, slipmats, record sleeves. Leave it as clean as you found it.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is St. Vincent's Triangle?

It's a small triangular park in Greenwich Village, Manhattan, bounded by Seventh Avenue, Greenwich Avenue and West 12th Street. Since December 2016 it has been home to the New York City AIDS Memorial, across the street from the former St. Vincent's Hospital.

### Who designed the NYC AIDS Memorial?

The white steel triangular canopy was designed by Studio a+i. The artist Jenny Holzer arranged passages from Walt Whitman's "Song of Myself" in the granite paving around the central water feature.

### Can you play music at the NYC AIDS Memorial?

It's a public park, but it's also a place of mourning and reflection. A very quiet, battery-powered session away from the fountain and the inscriptions felt respectful to me. Amplified sound in NYC parks technically requires a permit, so keep it small and stop right away if anyone asks.

### What gear did you use?

A battery-powered Numark PT01 Scratch portable turntable with its built-in speaker, plus a scratch record. Everything fits in one bag.
