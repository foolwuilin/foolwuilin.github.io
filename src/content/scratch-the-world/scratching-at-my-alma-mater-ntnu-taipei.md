On 23 September 2017 I carried a battery-powered Numark PT01 back to the one campus in Taipei that already had my name on a diploma — National Taiwan Normal University, the school everyone here just calls Shida (師大) — and cut a few bars under the palms by the old red-brick gate on Heping East Road while the last of the summer cicadas roared overhead. Coming back to scratch at the place where I earned my Master's felt less like sightseeing and more like walking back into an old room inside my own head.

The caption I posted that day was short and a little personal: “National Taiwan Normal University, which I got my Master's from.” That was the whole reason this stop mattered. Most pins on this map are places I merely passed through; this one raised me. Setting a turntable down on ground I used to cross every single day — a whole degree's worth of late nights and library shifts behind me — turned an ordinary humid afternoon into something quietly sentimental.

## The campus that trains Taiwan's teachers

National Taiwan Normal University — 國立臺灣師範大學, universally shortened to Shida (師大) — is Taiwan's oldest teacher-training university and one of its great arts institutions, sitting right in the heart of Da'an District. Its most famous College of Music and its fine-arts departments give the place a very different flavour from the science-and-engineering campuses across town: on any given day the crowd is an eclectic mix of trainee high-school teachers, working local artists, and the international students who flood in for the university's huge Mandarin Training Center. It is a campus with music and language baked into its walls, which is exactly why bringing a turntable here never felt like a stretch.

## In through the gate on Heping East Road

The front gate opens straight onto the traffic of Heping East Road (和平東路), and the moment you step through it you meet the building everyone photographs: the old Administration Building, put up in 1928 during the Japanese colonial era. Its deep red-brick façade, arched porticos and quietly grand roofline are the closest thing Shida has to a signature, and in the low golden-hour light of a September afternoon the brick almost glows. Palm trees line the way in, throwing long fronded shadows across the pavement, and the whole entrance has the settled, dignified calm of a place that has been teaching people for the better part of a century.

## A neighbourhood called Shida

Step back out of the gate and you are in one of Taipei's most beloved student quarters. Shida Road curls away from the campus and pours south into the Shida Night Market — quieter now than it was a decade ago, but still a warren of bohemian cafés, vintage boutiques and cheap, brilliant student food. Ten minutes' walk west drops you at Guting MRT, and a short stroll east opens onto the green sprawl of Da'an Forest Park. Living inside that little triangle for a couple of years, I came to think of the whole neighbourhood as an extension of the campus — books and beats and bubble tea, all within a few blocks.

## A sticky late-September afternoon

Late September in Taipei is not autumn; it is the long, damp tail of the subtropical summer. The heat had baked the pavement all day, and the humidity wrapped around me like a warm, wet towel the second I stopped moving. The air was a whole city in one breath: rain-washed concrete, a thread of scooter exhaust, and — drifting over from the night-market stalls just beginning to fire up — the unmistakable smell of frying garlic. Sunlight came down through the palm fronds in sharp, moving patches against the red brick. And over everything sat the sound of the season: the deafening, metallic thrum of cicadas clinging to the big twisting banyans, loud enough to ride right on top of a beat, laced with the chatter of students spilling out of their afternoon lectures.

## Setting up the PT01 under the banyans

I found a spot the way I always do — a low wall in the shade of the banyans, tucked back from the heavy pedestrian flow near the front gate, with the little Numark PT01 balanced on top and a short stack of 7-inch records beside it. I kept the volume conversational, out of respect for a campus where classes were still running, and treated it exactly as what it was: a public practice session, tip hat left in the bag. The cuts, as usual, humbled me — sweaty hands, an uneven surface, and cicadas fighting me for the high end. Nobody minded. A lone guy with a tiny turntable reads here as a hobbyist or a club member rehearsing, not a nuisance. Taipei does run a formal street-artist permit system for anyone busking for tips, and if a security guard had asked me to move, the only right answer is a smile, a quick “bù hǎo yì si,” and a quiet pack-up — but that afternoon the campus just let me be.

## Why my old grad school earns a place on the map

It might sound odd to scratch at a university instead of a landmark, but Shida is exactly the right kind of place. Because of the College of Music, the neighbourhood has spent decades as Taipei's bohemian, intellectual heart — hidden vinyl shops down the alleys, indie cafés, a long quiet history of underground live music. A hip-hop beat and the sharp skrrt of a crossfader don't fight that culture; they plug straight into its crate-digging, self-taught DNA. And there was the personal part, too: this was the one stop on the whole map where I wasn't leaving a sound in a stranger's city, but adding one back to a place that had already given me a degree.

## Frequently asked questions

### Where was this filmed?

On the main campus of National Taiwan Normal University (師大) in Da'an District, Taipei — just inside the front gate on Heping East Road (和平東路), by the 1928 red-brick Administration Building, a short walk from the Shida Night Market and Guting MRT.

### Is National Taiwan Normal University the same as National Taiwan University?

No — they're two different schools, and locals never mix them up. National Taiwan Normal University (師大, NTNU) is the teacher-training and arts university on Heping East Road; National Taiwan University (台大, NTU) is the larger, science-heavy campus over by Gongguan and Roosevelt Road. I scratched at 台大 a week before this and at 師大 here — different gates, different neighbourhoods, same subtropical cicadas.

### Can you set up and scratch around the NTNU campus?

Mostly, yes — with common sense. The campus is open to the public, and a small battery-powered turntable reads as personal practice rather than a performance. Taipei runs a formal “street artist” permit system for buskers who want to collect tips in designated spots, so leave the tip jar packed, keep the volume down near classrooms, don't block walkways, and if security asks you to move, move.

### What gear did you use?

A battery-powered Numark PT01 Scratch — the same little portable deck I've carried through this whole series, chosen because it runs on batteries and fits in a bag, so any bench, wall or campus step can become a booth.
