On 13 October 2017, a few weeks after flying back to New York from a run of sessions in Taipei, I set a battery-powered Numark PT01 Scratch down on a picnic table in McCarren Park — the big green seam between Williamsburg and Greenpoint in North Brooklyn — and spent a bright autumn afternoon trading cuts with two fellow NYC scratch DJs, @turtlescratch and @talamarc.

The caption I posted that day was about as short as they come: "Fun session. Cutting with @turtlescratch and @talamarc." That really was the whole plan. No landmark to pose in front of, no event, no crowd — just three people with portable decks, one shared beat, and the kind of low-stakes afternoon session that reminds you why you started doing this in the first place.

## North Brooklyn's shared backyard

McCarren Park is roughly 35 acres of lawns, ball fields and paths sitting right on the line where Williamsburg turns into Greenpoint. It opened in 1906, and it doesn't feel like one tidy park so much as a handful of parcels stitched together by the streets that cut straight through it — Bedford Avenue, Driggs Avenue, Lorimer Street and North 12th Street, with Nassau Avenue running along the Greenpoint side. On any decent afternoon it works as the neighbourhood's backyard: a running track, soccer and baseball fields, handball walls, dogs everywhere, picnic blankets on the grass, and people who have simply come to sit on a bench for an hour.

Getting there is easy. I took the L train to Bedford Avenue and walked north up Bedford, past coffee shops, record stores and vintage racks, until the buildings fell back and the trees opened up. If you're coming from Queens or the other end of Brooklyn, the G to Nassau Avenue drops you right on the park's northern edge.

## A pool with a music history

The park's landmark is the McCarren Play Center — the huge WPA-era pool complex that opened in 1936 behind a red-brick arched entrance you can't really miss. Its story is a very New York one: the pool closed in the mid-1980s and sat empty for about two decades, and in the mid-2000s the drained basin was turned into an outdoor concert space, with indie and rock bands playing to crowds standing where the water used to be. It reopened as a public pool in 2012.

Knowing that made our little session feel less random. This corner of Brooklyn has a long habit of turning odd outdoor spaces into stages, and the whole neighbourhood still carries a DIY music streak — record diggers, beatmakers, bedroom producers, buskers. By mid-October the pool had closed for the season, but the benches and tables around the park were still full of people making their own noise. Three portable decks on a picnic table fit right in.

## Mid-October light and the sound of the park

October is my favourite month to do this in New York. The summer humidity is finally gone, the afternoon sun sits low and golden, and the big London plane trees are just starting to drop crisp yellow-brown leaves onto the paths. You can see it in the clip — hard, slanting light raking across the table, the deck half in shadow, the park glowing behind us.

The soundscape was already busy before we added anything to it: runners pounding round the track, the thud of a ball from the fields, dogs barking, conversations drifting over from the benches, now and then a car rolling along Lorimer with the windows down and the bass up. A crossfader's click and a stab of a scratch sample just becomes one more layer in all that. People don't stare for long — someone slows down, watches a couple of cuts, nods, and keeps walking.

## Cutting in a three-way cipher

Cutting with other scratch DJs is a completely different feeling from scratching alone. With three portables on one table, you take turns over a shared beat: one person runs a phrase, the next answers it, the third layers something on top. It's much more conversation than performance. And you pick things up from each other without trying — a different way to hold the record, a fader rhythm you hadn't thought of, the way someone resets their sample cleanly between phrases.

The best part is the pace. There's no audience to play to and no clock, so you can stop mid-cut, laugh at a train wreck, swap a record and go again. That's the part of portablism I love most: it turns a picnic table in a public park into a practice room you share with friends.

## Keeping it park-friendly

In New York City parks, properly amplified sound — speakers pushed loud enough to fill a lawn — generally needs a sound device permit. A portable scratch session is a different thing if you keep it small: battery-powered decks, a modest little speaker, volume low enough that it doesn't carry much past your own table.

Our unwritten rules were simple. Pick a table away from the playgrounds and families spreading out on the grass, keep cables tidy and off the path, no tip jar, and happily turn it down or move if anyone asks. Treat the park like everyone's backyard — because it is — and people meet you with curiosity rather than complaints.

## Frequently asked questions

### Where was this filmed?

At McCarren Park in North Brooklyn, on the border of Williamsburg and Greenpoint — a roughly 35-acre park crossed by Bedford Avenue, Driggs Avenue, Lorimer Street and North 12th Street, a short walk north of the Bedford Avenue L station or right by the Nassau Avenue G station.

### Can you play portable turntables in a New York City park?

Quiet, battery-powered practice is usually fine. Loud amplified sound in NYC parks generally requires a sound device permit, so keep the setup small and the volume at conversation level, stay clear of playgrounds and organised events, and be ready to turn down or move on if a Parks officer or a neighbour asks.

### What does "cutting with" someone mean in scratch DJing?

Cutting is scratching and chopping a sample in rhythm using the record and the crossfader. "Cutting with" other DJs means trading scratch phrases over the same beat — a musical back-and-forth where each person takes a turn and then answers or builds on what the last one played.

### What gear did you use?

A battery-powered Numark PT01 Scratch — the same portable deck I've carried through this whole series — with a short stack of 7-inch records. It fits in a shoulder bag, which is exactly why a park picnic table can turn into a booth.
