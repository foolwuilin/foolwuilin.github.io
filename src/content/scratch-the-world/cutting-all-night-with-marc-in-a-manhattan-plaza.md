On the night of 1 November 2017 I set a battery-powered Numark PT01 Scratch on a concrete table in a lit-up Manhattan plaza and spent hours trading cuts with my friend Marc (@talamarc) — cold hands, string-light glare and nobody in a hurry. My caption said it all: "Hanging out with @talamarc all night long." I only tagged the post "New York, New York", so I'll tell you what that night felt like rather than pretend to give you a street address.

## An all-night hang with Marc

Most Scratch the World stops are a quick set-up, a few bars and a pack-up. This one was different. Marc and I weren't chasing a landmark; we were just two portablists with a turntable each and a whole night to burn. We'd already spent a lot of evenings that autumn cutting in New York parks, but by the first of November the parks had gone cold and dark early, so we went looking for somewhere with light, a table and a bit of shelter from the wind.

That's how we ended up in a plaza: hard stone underfoot, built-in concrete benches and tables, a few bare trees overhead and strings of café lights glowing above us. The video I posted is mostly glare and silhouette, and honestly that's how I remember it too.

## Why a Manhattan plaza works at night

Manhattan has a lot of plazas that are public in everything but name. Many are privately owned public spaces (POPS), created under the city's 1961 zoning rules, where a developer got extra floor area in exchange for giving the street a plaza with seating. You'll find them tucked in front of office towers all over Midtown and Lower Manhattan, and they're a gift if you carry a portable turntable: a flat table at exactly the right height, power-free, with built-in lighting.

The big public squares do the same job in a different way. Places like Union Square, the little triangles along Broadway and the pedestrian plazas around Greenwich Village stay busy late, so you always have a handful of curious passers-by and no sense of being somewhere sketchy.

## What the night actually felt like

Early November in New York is the first week you really feel winter coming. After dark it drops to somewhere around 45°F (7°C) and keeps falling, and a stone table sucks the warmth straight out of your forearms. Your fingers stiffen after about fifteen minutes, which is a real problem when scratching depends on a loose, quick crossfader hand. I was fiddling with my sleeves between phrases more than I'd like to admit.

The light was the second thing: warm amber street lamps fighting white LED lobby lights, with the string bulbs throwing little halos on the PT01's glossy faceplate. The third was the sound. Under the cuts there's always the city — a distant subway rumble coming up through the sidewalk grates, the hiss of a bus on a cross street, steam venting somewhere — and it fills in the gaps between your beats better than any sample. Now and then a halal cart on the corner sent over a smell of cumin and grilled meat that made it very hard to stay on the decks.

## How street music fits in

Manhattan has a long tradition of people playing music in public, from subway-platform bands to drummers on Washington Square. A small battery-powered turntable at conversational volume sits comfortably on the quiet end of that spectrum. Most of the time people either ignore you or watch for a minute and give you a nod; nobody at that hour was in the mood to complain.

The unspoken rules are simple: don't set up right on top of a subway entrance or another performer, keep your kit small enough to scoop into one bag if someone asks you to move, and keep the volume where a friend could talk next to you without shouting.

## Etiquette: public versus privately owned plazas

There's a real difference between a city-owned plaza and a POPS. Formally, amplified sound on city streets and plazas can require a permit, which is why I keep everything battery-powered and quiet — it's a personal practice session in public, not a show. On a privately owned plaza, building security can ask you to leave, usually later in the night, and the polite answer is always "of course" and a quick pack-up. We never had any trouble, but it's a good reason to keep your set-up fast.

## How I'd set up for a night session

- Bring fingerless gloves, because stiff fingers ruin everything.
- Use a stable, flat table or bench; keep the PT01 steady with a folded slipmat or cloth.
- Charge everything, and bring spare batteries — the cold drains them quickly.
- Keep the volume low and the set-up small.
- Go with a friend. Having Marc next to me turned a freezing night into one of the warmest memories of that autumn.

## Frequently asked questions

### Where did you scratch on 1 November 2017?
It was a lit-up public plaza in Manhattan. I tagged the post only as "New York, New York", so I can't point to an exact address, but the stone benches, built-in tables and string lights are typical of Manhattan's public and privately owned plazas.

### Can you play a portable turntable in a New York plaza at night?
A small, battery-powered, low-volume set is generally tolerated, but amplified sound can technically need a permit, and security on privately owned plazas may ask you to move along. Stay polite, stay quiet and pack up fast if asked.

### How do you keep your hands warm when scratching outside in November?
Fingerless gloves, warm sleeves and short breaks. Cold stone tables drain heat quickly, so a folded cloth under your forearms helps too.

### What gear did you use?
A battery-powered Numark PT01 Scratch, with the sound kept at a low, conversational level.
