On 4 October 2017 I carried a battery-powered Numark PT01 Scratch into Ximending (西門町) — the pedestrian maze in Taipei's Wanhua District that everyone calls the "Harajuku of Taipei" — and cut a few bars right in the neon crush, with claw-machine jingles, boba-stand cocktail shakers and duelling Mandopop tracks all fighting my crossfader for the air.

The caption I posted that day was pure Ximending: *"The mural's interesting. Why rainbow pants. 😂😂😂😂"* I'd been staring at one of the district's painted walls, but the real riot of colour was underfoot — the giant rainbow crosswalk just outside the MRT — and dropping a needle there felt like scratching in the loudest, most crowded room in the whole city.

## The Harajuku of Taipei

Ximending is the undisputed heart of Taipei's youth culture: indie fashion, anime and manga fandom, tattoo studios, sneaker shops, cheap street eats and relentless commerce, all packed into a web of car-free lanes in the old Wanhua District. It was Taipei's first dedicated pedestrian shopping zone, and decades later it is still the place teenagers, tourists and subcultures of every stripe come to see and be seen. Bringing a turntable here was never going to feel out of place — if anything, one more source of noise barely registers.

## Where all the streets meet

The district's nervous system is that block of pedestrian-only lanes anchored by the intersection of Hanzhong Street (漢中街) and Wuchang Street (武昌街). Wuchang is lined with the big cinema complexes that made Ximending Taipei's movie district; Hanzhong is the main north–south artery that the foot traffic pours down. Almost everyone arrives the same way I did — up the stairs from Ximen MRT Station Exit 6 — and steps straight onto the "No. 6 Rainbow," an enormous rainbow crosswalk painted directly across the asphalt, half photo magnet and half symbol of Taiwan's stance on LGBTQ+ rights. That splash of colour underfoot was the "rainbow" my caption was laughing about.

## The Red House across Chengdu Road

A few steps south, across Chengdu Road (成都路), stands the Red House (西門紅樓, Ximen Honglou) — a striking octagonal red-brick landmark built in 1908 as Taiwan's first public market, and now a small theatre wrapped in a weekend creative bazaar and a cluster of open-air terrace bars. After a block of LED billboards it lands like a full stop: a century-old brick octagon holding its ground against all that neon. Setting up within sight of it, I liked the contrast — the oldest thing on the street and the newest sound on it, in the same frame.

## A whole city in one breath

Playing here means competing with a wall of sensory input. The October air still carried the damp weight of the subtropical summer, and it arrived layered with smells: the sharp, fermented tang of fried stinky tofu, the caramelised sizzle of blowtorched beef cubes from a night-market stall, sweet clouds of bubble tea. Overhead, multi-storey LED billboards and towering neon signs cycled through cosmetics ads and mobile-game trailers. And the soundscape was pure chaos — the tinny jingles of endless claw-machine arcades, the rattle of cocktail shakers at the boba stands, and rival Mandopop tracks blasting out of facing storefronts. A crossfader has to work hard to be heard over all of that, which is exactly why it sounds so good when it cuts through.

## Setting up the PT01 in the crowd

Street performers are part of Ximending's DNA. On any given evening you share the asphalt with hip-hop dance crews, acoustic guitarists, diabolo (Chinese yo-yo) jugglers and the occasional living statue. A compact, battery-powered rig — the little PT01 and a short crate of 7-inch records — is built for exactly this transient energy; the percussive click of the crossfader and the tear of a scratch routine cut clean through the ambient roar. The coveted pitches are the wide plaza right outside Exit 6, the central node where Hanzhong meets Wuchang, and the calmer, arts-leaning plaza in front of the Red House. I kept mine modest: volume just loud enough to register, tip bag zipped shut, cables tucked in so nobody in that shoulder-to-shoulder crowd tripped. Taipei does run a formal street-performer permit (街頭藝人證), district managers and police do patrol, and volume wars are frowned on — so the honest move is to stay light on your feet and pack up with a smile if you're asked to move.

## Why Ximending earns a place on the map

Some stops on this map are quiet landmarks that ask you to lower your voice. Ximending is the opposite: it dares you to be heard. Scratching here wasn't about a pretty backdrop — it was about planting a beat in the single densest, brightest, noisiest square kilometre in Taipei and seeing whether the cuts could hold their own against the claw machines and the Mandopop. That's the whole appeal of portable turntablism in a place like this: the city is already playing at full volume, and all you're doing is adding one more instrument to it.

## Frequently asked questions

### Where was this filmed?

In Ximending (西門町), the pedestrian shopping district in Taipei's Wanhua District — the car-free lanes around Ximen MRT Station Exit 6, near the "No. 6 Rainbow" crosswalk and the 1908 Red House (西門紅樓), at the Hanzhong Street and Wuchang Street intersection.

### Do you need a permit to busk in Ximending?

For anything real, yes. Taipei runs a formal street-performer permit system (街頭藝人證), and high-traffic spots are allocated by registration or lottery, with secondary pitches claimed by arriving early. A tiny battery deck kept quiet with no tip jar reads as personal practice, but the legal way to perform for tips is to hold the permit, take a designated spot, keep your cables clear of the crowd, and pack up politely if a district manager or officer asks you to.

### What is the rainbow crosswalk in Ximending?

It's the "No. 6 Rainbow," a large rainbow-striped crosswalk painted across the road right outside Ximen MRT Exit 6. It doubles as a hugely popular photo spot and a symbol of Taiwan's LGBTQ+ rights — Taiwan was the first place in Asia to legalise same-sex marriage. That crosswalk is the rainbow my caption was joking about.

### What gear did you use?

A battery-powered Numark PT01 Scratch — the same little portable deck I've carried through this whole series, chosen because it runs on batteries and fits in a bag, so any step, wall or plaza edge can become a booth.
