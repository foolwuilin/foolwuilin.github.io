Not every stop on Scratch the World is a monument. Some are just a good stretch of sidewalk, a cold afternoon, and a new toy to try out. On 18 November 2017 that stretch was Mercer Street, right beside the long green sliver of Mercer Playground on the NYU edge of Greenwich Village — and the new toy was a looper app I'd just loaded up and wanted to run with my portable turntable. I'm DJ FOOL Wuilin (吳奕霖), and this one was less a performance than a quiet practice session in one of the Village's best-kept corners.

## A green sliver on Mercer Street

Mercer Playground is barely there if you're not looking for it — a narrow ribbon of asphalt and London plane trees pressed along the western edge of Mercer Street between Bleecker and West 3rd. Its odd shoelace shape is a leftover of a plan that never happened: the strip sat half-empty for decades, reserved for a street-widening scheme, until neighbours clawed it back in the early '90s and it reopened as a park in 1999. The thing I love is the black wrought-iron fence around it — all iron spirals and little cut-out silhouettes, with U.S. state names and state birds worked right into the metal. I didn't set up inside, though: in New York an adult can't just hang around inside a fenced children's playground without a kid along, so I planted myself on the wide sidewalk just outside the rail, turntable on my knees.

## The seam where the Village meets SoHo

Stand on that sidewalk and you're sitting on an architectural fault line. Directly behind me, the two 17-storey concrete slabs of Washington Square Village loomed up with their red, yellow and blue balcony panels; across the one-way street sat 250 Mercer, a hulking 19th-century warehouse turned lofts, next to NYU's Courant math building. A block south, past Bleecker, rise I.M. Pei's three raw-concrete towers around a lawn with a big Picasso sculpture; one more block and West Houston Street drops you straight into SoHo's cast-iron canyons and Belgian-block cobblestones. Washington Square Park and its marble arch are a five-minute walk north. Just south of me that afternoon was a giant construction pit — NYU had flattened its old Coles gym and was digging the foundations of the glassy new building everyone called the "Zipper."

## Running the new looper

The reason I'd come, though, was the app. I built a short loop, let it run, and started cutting over the top with the PT01 Scratch — chopping the loop, flipping little phrases backwards, leaning on the dry click-clack of the fader more than on volume. There's something addictive about a fresh piece of gear in a new spot: you stop thinking about who's watching and just chase the next idea. Out there with cold fingers on a colder platter, a looper turns a solo practice into a little one-man band.

## A mid-November afternoon, exactly

By about half three the sun had already slid down behind the Hudson, and the big slab to the west threw the whole strip into a flat blue shadow while a thin band of amber still clung to the top-floor cornices across the street. (The gates get locked at 6 all winter.) The sound was its own strange pocket: one block south, Houston Street was a non-stop river of cabs, air brakes and trucks banging over steel road plates; the construction site clanked and beeped until the crews knocked off; and right next to me, dry plane leaves scraped across the asphalt like sandpaper every time the wind funnelled up from SoHo, mixed with kids on Razor scooters. Cold iron rail, gritty green bench paint, the chill of vinyl and plastic under stiffening hands. It smelled of wet leaves, diesel off Houston, and espresso drifting out of the Angelika a block down. Students streamed past in puffers and purple NYU lanyards, barely glancing over.

## The Village's quiet practice pocket

This is what makes Mercer feel different. Two blocks north, Washington Square Park is a busking bloodsport — jazz horns, bucket drummers and chess hustlers all fighting for the same air and the same tourists. Mercer isn't a pitch; nobody's out here working a tip bucket. It's more like an open-air practice room. On any given day you'll pass an NYU drama kid muttering a monologue on a bench, a jazz student fingering scales on an un-amplified sax, and — that day — me, cutting a scratch record on a battery-powered deck. You're also standing on real hip-hop ground: WNYU broadcast from up the block, Village record stores were a short walk away, and downtown's boom-bap history soaked into these streets long ago. Out here a portable turntable doesn't earn a confused stare — it earns a knowing nod from some Village lifer in a Kangol.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is Mercer Playground?

Mercer Playground is a narrow public park running along the western side of Mercer Street between Bleecker and West 3rd Street, on the Greenwich Village–NoHo border by NYU. It was reclaimed from a long-vacant strip and opened in 1999, and it's known for its snaking skate path, London plane trees, and a decorative iron fence stamped with U.S. state names and birds.

### Can you play a turntable or perform on Mercer Street?

Quietly, yes. Keep it simple: set up outside the playground fence (adults aren't allowed inside a fenced kids' playground in NYC without a child), don't block the sidewalk, skip the tip jar, and keep a small battery speaker at conversational volume. A single person cutting low on a portable deck reads as "someone enjoying the afternoon," not an event — technically, amplified sound needs a permit, but nobody minds a palm-sized speaker you can barely hear ten feet away over Houston Street.

### What gear do you need for this?

Very little. Mine was a battery-powered **Numark PT01 Scratch** — a portable scratch turntable — plus a scratch record and, that day, a looper app to build a quick loop to cut over. No mixer, no power outlet, no booth. It all rides on your shoulder.

### Where's the best nearby spot to see street performers?

Walk five minutes north to Washington Square Park. Around the fountain and under the arch you'll find the Village's loudest, most competitive busking — jazz bands, bucket drummers, guitarists and the famous chess players — a complete contrast to Mercer's quiet.
