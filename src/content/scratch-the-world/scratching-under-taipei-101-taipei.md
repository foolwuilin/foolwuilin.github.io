A week into scratching my way around Taipei I'd already hit a temple, a gadget mall, a memorial-hall plaza and a zoo. There was really only one landmark left that *is* the city in most people's minds — and this was the day I finally set up under it. The caption I posted was as short as it gets: "Under Taipei 101."

## The tower that stands in for the whole city

At 508 metres, Taipei 101 wasn't the tallest building on earth any more by 2017 — Dubai's Burj Khalifa had taken that crown back in 2010 — but it still owns the Taipei skyline completely. It's built to look like a stalk of bamboo, eight flared segments stacked one on the next (eight being the lucky number in Chinese culture), all sheathed in that pale blue-green glass. High up, slung between the 87th and 92nd floors, hangs the thing I love most about it: a 660-tonne golden steel sphere, a tuned mass damper that swings like a giant pendulum to steady the tower against typhoons and earthquakes. Tourists ride some of the fastest lifts in the world up to the observatory on floors 88 to 91 to stand next to that gold ball. I stayed at ground level, which felt like the right altitude for a shoulder-bag turntable anyway.

## Setting up in the Xinyi plaza

The base of 101 sits in Xinyi — say it "Shin-yee" — Taipei's gleaming financial and shopping district, and you barely touch the actual street there: a web of elevated skywalks stitches the tower to mega-malls like Shin Kong Mitsukoshi, ATT 4 FUN and the Vieshow cinemas. The plaza itself is polished granite, with Robert Indiana's big red **LOVE** sculpture out front pulling a permanent queue of people angling for the same photo. I found a low ledge, balanced the PT01 on it, and dropped the needle with the tower going straight up into the haze behind me. A few days earlier I'd scratched on the stone apron of the Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hall just to the northwest; now Elephant Mountain sat green and steep off to the east, a wall of jungle standing behind all that glass.

## A September afternoon of heat and wind

September in Taipei is still deep summer — thirty-something degrees, air so humid you're damp the second you stop moving, and a sky forever half-threatening one of those sudden afternoon downpours. Every time a mall door slid open the air-conditioning rolled out like a physical wall. The real enemy, though, was the wind: a tower this tall makes its own weather at the base, and gusts kept whipping across the plaza and threatening to skate my slipmat and lift the light little 7-inch clean off the platter. Around me the city hummed — the low idle of a hundred 125cc scooters at the intersection swelling to a swarm every time the lights changed, the cuckoo-chirp of the pedestrian crossings, the smell of rain-baked asphalt and scooter exhaust cut with the starchy steam of xiaolongbao from the Din Tai Fung down in the basement. By dusk the sky went violet and 101 lit up in its colour of the day.

## Cutting quietly under the giants

I kept the PT01's little internal speaker low — partly courtesy, partly self-preservation — and scratched small and clean, because between the light platter, the featherweight tonearm and those gusts, anything too aggressive would have thrown the needle straight out of the groove. Xinyi is actually the headquarters of Taiwanese street performance: licensed buskers (街頭藝人) audition for a city permit and get their own chalked squares along the walkway over to Shin Kong Mitsukoshi. I had no permit and no square, so this was pure guerrilla work — the sort that only survives on politeness. A little Taipei crowd gathered exactly the way they always do here: a shy, quiet semicircle standing about three metres back, phones up, watching intently and clapping softly when a routine landed, but never once crowding in. The one thing to respect is the security — sharp suits and earpieces who guard the tower's frontage and move fast on anything that looks commercial — so the rule is stay small, stay non-commercial, keep the walkway clear, and be ready to smile and drift off the moment a uniform heads your way.

## Why Taipei 101 earned a pin

Scratch the World has always been about carrying a turntable into places that would never book a DJ and leaving a little sound behind. A corporate glass megatower, the single most photographed thing in the country, with a hip-hop crossfader chattering away at its feet — that clash is the entire point. It all still packed into one bag slung over a shoulder, ready to appear for ten minutes and vanish again. Manhattan started this whole thing back in June; three months later, under the bamboo tower in Xinyi, the Taipei half of the map finally had its centrepiece.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can you busk or DJ at Taipei 101?

Not without a permit. Street performance in Taiwan is licensed — buskers (街頭藝人) audition with the city and play only in designated zones, and Xinyi has its own marked busking spots along the skywalk to Shin Kong Mitsukoshi. A quick, unlicensed portable session at the plaza only works if you keep it tiny and quiet, stay out of the foot traffic, avoid anything that looks commercial, and pack up the moment 101's security asks.

### What is Taipei 101?

Taipei 101 is a 508-metre supertall skyscraper in the Xinyi District of Taipei, Taiwan — the tallest building in the world from 2004 until 2010. It's shaped like a segmented bamboo stalk, houses an observatory on floors 88 to 91 reached by some of the fastest lifts on the planet, and is famous for the giant 660-tonne golden tuned mass damper that steadies it against typhoons and earthquakes.

### Is it hard to scratch on a portable turntable there?

The wind is the hard part. A supertall tower funnels strong gusts across its plaza, and a battery portable like the PT01 has a light platter and a light tonearm, so you scratch smaller and cleaner than usual to keep the needle in the groove — and you keep the speaker low so you don't draw a crowd or the tower's security.

### What gear did you use?

A Numark PT01 Scratch — a battery-powered portable turntable built for scratch DJs — with a portable fader and a scratch record. Because it runs on batteries and lives in a shoulder bag, it can set up on a ledge anywhere, which is the only reason a session at the foot of Taipei 101 is even possible.
