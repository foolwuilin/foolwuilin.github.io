On 21 October 2017 I sat on a planter ledge at the edge of Rockefeller Center's Lower Plaza, balanced a battery-powered Numark PT01 Scratch on my knees, and cut a few bars under a ring of international flags with 30 Rock towering straight above me. My caption that day was just "I'm a tourist." — and for one afternoon in Midtown, that was exactly the point.

## Why I played tourist in my own city

By October 2017 I'd been living in New York long enough to walk right past Rockefeller Center without looking up. That's what locals do: you cut across Sixth Avenue, dodge the crowds on 49th or 50th Street, and keep moving. A week after a relaxed picnic-table session in Brooklyn, I decided to do the opposite — go stand where the tourists stand, look where they look, and leave a scratch behind instead of a selfie.

So the caption wasn't a joke at anyone else's expense. It was a joke at mine. I had headphones on, a wide-angle camera pointed up at the skyscrapers, and a tiny turntable in my lap. If you'd walked by, you'd have assumed I was visiting from somewhere else. Honestly, that afternoon, I sort of was.

## Under the flags and the Art Deco giants

Rockefeller Center isn't one building — it's a whole Art Deco complex of around nineteen buildings, put up by John D. Rockefeller Jr. during the Great Depression, filling the blocks between Fifth and Sixth Avenues from 48th to 51st Street. The centrepiece is 30 Rockefeller Plaza, the 70-storey tower that rises in those long, stepped limestone lines, with the Top of the Rock observation deck at its crown.

I set up at the rim of the sunken Lower Plaza, where a ring of flagpoles lines the edge at street level. If you look at the thumbnail from that day, that's the frame: flags snapping in every colour on the left, the tower going straight up behind my shoulder, and a steady river of people leaning on the railing to look down into the plaza. Down below, the gilded Prometheus statue floats over the fountain, catching the sun like it's lit from inside. It's a ridiculous backdrop for a four-inch record — which is exactly why I wanted it.

## Late October, just before the holiday rush

Timing made a big difference. The famous Christmas tree doesn't arrive until mid-November, so in late October the plaza still belongs to autumn. The Channel Gardens — the gently sloping promenade that runs in from Fifth Avenue between the British Empire Building and La Maison Française (hence the "channel") — were dressed in fall plantings, and the long pools down the middle reflected a clear, pale sky.

The ice rink in the Lower Plaza had already opened for the season, so under my scratches there was a whole second soundtrack: skate blades scraping and hissing across fresh ice, pop music drifting up from the rink speakers, kids shrieking as they wobbled around the edge. Add the traffic on the avenues, a tour guide's voice somewhere behind me, and the smell of honey-roasted nuts floating in from the street carts, and you've got a Midtown mix no producer could build on purpose. It was one of those unusually mild October afternoons, too — warm enough to sit outside in a long-sleeve shirt and not think about gloves.

## Scratching where performers aren't the norm

Here's the thing you need to know before you copy this: Rockefeller Center is private property, not a public park. Unlike Washington Square or Union Square, it isn't a place where buskers set up for the day. The plaza has its own security team, and anything that looks like a show — an amp, a tip jar, a crowd blocking the walkway — gets moved along quickly.

That's why the portable rig matters so much. A PT01 runs on batteries, the whole setup fits on your lap, and with headphones on the sound barely leaves your own space. I wasn't performing for anyone; I was a guy on a ledge with a record, like the dozens of people around me with cameras and coffee cups. Nobody stopped, nobody complained, and a few people glanced over, clocked the turntable, and smiled. That's the magic of portablism at a spot like this: you can take part in a landmark without taking it over.

## My quick-clip etiquette for landmark spots

After this session I kept a simple set of rules for private plazas and famous landmarks, and they've served me well ever since:

- **Keep your footprint tiny.** Only what fits on your lap or beside you on a ledge. No cases on the ground, no tripods in the walkway.
- **Keep the volume personal.** Headphones, or a speaker so quiet it only reaches you.
- **No tip jar, no signs.** If it looks like a paid performance, it isn't a snapshot anymore.
- **Make it short.** Film your clip, enjoy the moment, and pack up. It's a postcard, not a residency.
- **If security asks, smile and go.** On private property it's their call, and a friendly exit keeps the door open for the next portablist.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can you play music or busk at Rockefeller Center?

Rockefeller Center is privately owned and managed, so it's not an open busking spot like some public parks. Security generally stops anything that looks like an organised performance — amplifiers, tip jars, or crowds blocking the walkways. A quiet, headphone-level moment that looks like any other visitor enjoying the plaza is a very different thing, but if staff ask you to stop, pack up right away.

### Where exactly was this filmed?

At the edge of the Lower Plaza in Rockefeller Center, Midtown Manhattan, beside the ring of flagpoles, with 30 Rockefeller Plaza rising directly above. The complex spans 48th to 51st Street between Fifth and Sixth Avenues; St. Patrick's Cathedral is across Fifth Avenue and Radio City Music Hall is on the corner of Sixth Avenue and 50th Street.

### Is the Rockefeller Center ice rink open in October?

In 2017 the rink was already open by late October, well before the Christmas tree arrives in mid-November. Autumn is a nice window to visit — the rink is running and the Channel Gardens are in their fall look, but the holiday crowds haven't peaked yet.

### What gear did you use?

A battery-powered Numark PT01 Scratch portable turntable, a small record, and a pair of headphones. The whole kit sat on my lap, which is the only way I'd do it in a busy private plaza like this.
