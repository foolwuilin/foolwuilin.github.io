Twenty-four hours earlier I'd been crouched over the carpet at JFK, telling myself "before headed to Taiwan, let me crazy again," and cutting one last New York scratch before the gate. Now I was on the far side of the Pacific. I'm DJ FOOL Wuilin (吳奕霖), and after a whole summer of carrying a portable turntable into New York's parks, squares and departure halls, the flight had finally set me down where I'm actually from. Taiwan Taoyuan International Airport isn't a glamorous place to restart a project, but that's exactly why I liked it: Scratch the World had crossed an ocean, and its first pin on home soil was going to be right here, bag still on my shoulder, records still warm from the overhead bin.

## Wheels down after fifteen hours over the Pacific

The flight home from New York is a long, quiet unspooling — the better part of fifteen hours in the air, a meal, a bad movie, a few restless hours of half-sleep, and then the slow shuffle down TPE's long carpeted corridors with orchids lined up along the walls the way only a Taiwanese airport bothers to arrange them. Because I travel on a Taiwanese passport, immigration was almost anticlimactic: a few seconds at an automated e-Gate, a glance at the camera, and I was waved through before I'd fully woken up. Down at baggage claim a little quarantine beagle worked the carousel with its nose, checking bags for the fruit and meat you're not allowed to carry onto the island. By the time my record bag finally came around, I was home in every sense except the one still stuck somewhere back over the date line.

## The airport that is Taiwan's front door

Taoyuan is the island's main international gateway, sitting out on a flat, windswept coastal plain west of Taipei rather than in the city itself. In 2017 it ran on two very different terminals. Terminal 1 — the old 1979 building, reborn in a 2013 redesign by the Japanese architect Norihiko Dan — has a sweeping, bird-wing timber-slat ceiling over departures, though down at arrivals it stays low, heavy and almost brutalist, all thick concrete columns and slate-grey floor. Terminal 2, where most of the long-haul flights from North America come in, is the opposite: a bright, glassy hall of polished pale granite and exposed steel trusses, built for moving crowds efficiently. What made that particular summer feel like a turning point, though, was underground: the Taoyuan Airport MRT had only opened that March, and the vivid purple signs for the Express train now pointed you down to a sleek 35-minute ride straight into Taipei — no more queuing in the diesel haze for the old Kuo-Kuang airport bus.

## The sound and smell of being home

You know you've landed in Taiwan before you read a single sign. Every announcement is introduced by the same gentle four-note electronic chime, and then the message rolls through in Mandarin, Taiwanese Hokkien, Hakka and English, one after another, like a roll call of the island's languages. The light in the arrivals hall is flat, bright, unapologetic fluorescent; the air conditioning is cranked hard against the subtropical humidity outside, so the whole place feels crisp and faintly cold after the jet bridge. And underneath the clean smell of floor wax there it was — the warm, soy-and-five-spice smell of tea eggs (茶葉蛋) simmering in a pot at one of the 24-hour convenience stores tucked into the corners of the terminal. That smell, more than the passport stamp, is what tells a Taiwanese person they're actually home.

## Cutting a scratch where you're really not supposed to

An international airport is not a venue, and Taiwan takes that seriously. Street performance here is a licensed activity — buskers (街頭藝人) have to audition for a municipal permit and stick to designated spots — and the whole airport sits under the Aviation Police Bureau, where playing amplified music in the terminal simply isn't allowed. A guy unpacking a turntable in arrivals is an obvious anomaly, and if a beat had started leaking out of a speaker, someone in uniform would have been over politely but quickly. So I did it the only way that fits inside a Taiwanese public space: quietly, tucked into a dead corner, the volume barely there, headphones doing most of the listening, keeping myself small enough that the crowd could do what Taiwanese crowds do best with anything odd — the "polite ignore," a discreet sideways glance and a wide berth, nobody making a scene.

The other problem was the turntable itself. As I wrote that day, I was "doing my usual style, but it's not easy to do that on a portable because it requires better antiskip." On a full-size deck my heavier cuts sit fine in the groove; on the little battery-powered PT01, with its light platter and lightweight tonearm, the same moves make the needle want to hop straight out of the record. Half of scratching in a place like this is technique, and the other half is just wrestling your gear into behaving — which, honestly, is the whole spirit of portablism.

## The first Taiwan pin on the map

Looking back, the airport was the right place to plant the flag. Scratch the World was always about carrying a turntable into ordinary, awkward, in-between places and leaving a little sound behind, and there's nowhere more in-between than the arrivals hall of the airport you grew up flying out of. New York was where the project found its feet; Taoyuan was where it came home and kept going. Everything still fit in one bag I could sling over my shoulder and walk through customs with — and that portability is the entire point. The map had crossed the Pacific, and Taiwan was open.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can you busk or scratch inside Taoyuan Airport?

Not freely. Street performance in Taiwan is licensed — buskers (街頭藝人) audition for a permit and perform only in designated public zones — and Taoyuan Airport is patrolled by the Aviation Police Bureau, where playing amplified music inside the terminal is prohibited. The only realistic way to do anything like this is silently and to yourself: headphones on, no speaker, in a quiet out-of-the-way corner, keeping entirely to yourself.

### Why does a portable turntable skip when you scratch hard?

Because it's small and light. A portable like the Numark PT01 has a lighter platter and a lighter tonearm than a full-size DJ deck, so aggressive, fast scratch moves can knock the needle out of the groove — the "antiskip" problem. DJs usually fight it by adding weight to the headshell, increasing the tracking force a touch, or fitting a stabiliser ring, so the stylus stays planted while you cut.

### When did the Taoyuan Airport MRT open?

The Taoyuan Airport MRT began public service in March 2017, connecting the airport terminals to Taipei. Its Express service reaches Taipei Main Station in about 35 minutes, which made getting to and from TPE far easier than the older airport-bus routes.

### What gear did you use?

A Numark PT01 Scratch — a battery-powered portable turntable made for scratch DJs — plus a scratch record. Because it runs on batteries and fits in a shoulder bag, it clears customs and sets up anywhere, which is the only reason a session in an airport arrivals hall is even possible.
