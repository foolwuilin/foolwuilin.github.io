Every New York summer has to end somewhere, and mine ended in a departures hall at John F. Kennedy International Airport. I'm DJ FOOL Wuilin (吳奕霖), and after a season of carrying a portable turntable around the city — that first clip in Manhattan, a scratch picnic in Union Square, a new tonearm at Cooper Square, the Rock Steady Crew's 40th in Central Park — it was time to fly home to Taiwan. So before I did, I pulled the little Numark PT01 out of my carry-on one more time. "Before headed to Taiwan, let me crazy again," is exactly how I put it that day.

## One last scratch before the gate

There's a specific kind of restlessness that hits you at the airport a couple of hours before an international flight. You've checked your bag, you've cleared security, and now there's nothing to do but wait — that classic airport rhythm of hurry up and wait. For most people that means slumping into a vinyl gate chair next to a charging column and scrolling. For me it meant finding a quiet stretch of carpet, setting the PT01 on top of my bag, and cutting a few bars while the whole terminal churned around me. It felt like the right way to close out the trip: one more New York scratch, banked, before the city dropped below the wing.

## Getting to JFK: the Van Wyck and the AirTrain

If you've ever left New York from JFK, you know the airport makes you work for it before you even get inside. By car it means braving the Van Wyck Expressway — the I-678 — where a permanent slow crawl of yellow cabs, black town cars and shuttle buses bakes in the late-August heat coming off the concrete. The Queens around the airport is flat and industrial: warehouse roofs, logistics yards, and the marshland edge of Jamaica Bay off to the side.

Come in by transit and you ride the AirTrain instead, a driverless little train that rattles along on tracks above the roadways and stitches all the scattered terminals together. In the summer of 2017 that ride took you right past the old TWA Flight Center — Eero Saarinen's swooping 1962 "bird in flight" terminal at Terminal 5 — which back then was a fenced-off construction site wrapped in scaffolding and cranes, mid-transformation into the TWA Hotel. Half the fun of arriving is watching JFK's history get rebuilt around you on the way to the gate.

## A departures hall for the whole world

JFK isn't one airport so much as a loose collection of them. In 2017 it moved something like sixty million passengers a year, split across terminals that each have their own character and are run by different operators — it feels less like a single building and more like a cluster of little countries that happen to share a runway. It's the front door for trans-Pacific and transatlantic travel, so the mood is heavier than a domestic hub: people here aren't nipping to a meeting, they're emigrating, going home after years away, starting something big.

For a flight to Taipei the terminal depends on your airline. EVA Air runs out of the compact, utilitarian Terminal 1, its corridors crowded and a little dim, giant widebody jets parked right outside the glass. China Airlines flies from the sprawling Terminal 4, with its sail-like roof and huge glass walls, where clearing the security checkpoint funnels you straight into a bright duty-free hall before a genuinely long walk down the concourse past a Shake Shack to the far gates. Wherever you end up, the soundtrack is the same: the hypnotic squeak of polyurethane luggage wheels on terrazzo, overlapping PA announcements stacking English on top of Mandarin, French and more, and late-summer golden-hour light pouring through the plate glass to fight the fluorescents overhead.

## Scratching in a place that isn't built for it

An airport is about the least DJ-friendly public space there is, and that's part of why I wanted to do it. A few things I learned setting up a portable rig at JFK: security genuinely does not care about a turntable — the TSA sees far stranger things — but a bag densely packed with a crossfader, cables, a deck and a stack of 7-inch records can read as an odd, solid shape on the X-ray and earn you a friendly secondary bag check. Worth building in the extra minutes.

The bigger rule is about sound. Airports are high-stress, heavily patrolled spaces; the Port Authority Police and TSA are everywhere, and busking or playing music out loud is simply not allowed. The moment a beat leaks out of a speaker, someone will politely shut you down. So the only way portablism fits inside a terminal is in total isolation: headphones on, no speaker, needle down, and you disappear into your own little loop while the chaos of JFK swirls past. To anyone walking by, you're just a traveller hunched over a strange piece of plastic, silently pushing a record back and forth. That quiet, private version of scratching turned out to be its own kind of fun.

## Why the last pin mattered

Looking back, doing this at the airport was the perfect full stop for the New York chapter. Scratch the World was always about carrying a turntable into ordinary — and awkward — places and leaving a little sound behind, and there's nowhere more transitional than a departure gate. The whole idea is portability: if it doesn't fit in a bag I can sling over my shoulder and take through security, it doesn't come. This was the moment that portability got tested for real, on the way to a twelve-hour flight across the Pacific. New York was where Scratch the World found its feet; JFK was where it packed up and followed me home.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can you bring a portable turntable through airport security?

Yes. The TSA treats a battery-powered portable turntable like any other electronic device, so it's fine in your carry-on. Just expect that a bag densely packed with a deck, a crossfader, cables and a stack of vinyl can look unusual on the X-ray and may get pulled for a quick secondary bag check — leave a little extra time and pack it so it's easy to open.

### Can you actually play music or scratch inside an airport?

Not out loud. Airports like JFK are patrolled by the Port Authority Police and TSA, and busking or amplifying music in the terminal is prohibited — if sound leaks from a speaker, staff will shut it down fast. The workable way to practise is silently: headphones on, no external speaker, keeping entirely to yourself in a quiet corner or an empty gate area.

### Which JFK terminals fly to Taiwan?

As of 2017, EVA Air operated out of Terminal 1 and China Airlines out of Terminal 4, both flying nonstop from JFK to Taipei Taoyuan. The terminals are connected by the free AirTrain, so it's easy to move between them if you need to.

### What gear did you use at JFK?

A Numark PT01 Scratch — a battery-powered portable turntable built for scratch DJs — plus a scratch record. Because it runs on batteries, there's no outlet to hunt for, and the whole rig fits into a shoulder bag or carry-on, which is the entire reason a session like this is even possible.
