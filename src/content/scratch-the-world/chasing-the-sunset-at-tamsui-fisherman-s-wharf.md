I'd meant to chase surfers. The plan that day was to head up the coast out of Taipei, find a proper surf beach, and scratch with my toes in the sand. My own laziness had other ideas. I got as far as the end of the MRT line, looked at how far the real surf beaches still were, and quietly gave up on the whole idea — which is how I ended up at the nearest big stretch of open water instead: Tamsui Fisherman's Wharf, at the far western tip of northern Taiwan. It turned out to be one of the best accidents of the whole trip. I'm DJ FOOL Wuilin (吳奕霖).

## The beach I never reached

My caption that day was honest to a fault: I wanted a beach with surfers, but my laziness won. The Red Line runs all the way out to Tamsui, and by the time the train reached the last stop the surf beaches further up the north coast felt impossibly far. So I did the lazy, lucky thing and walked toward the water I could actually see. Tamsui Fisherman's Wharf isn't a surf beach — there's no sand, no break, no boards — but it sits at the exact place where the island runs out of land, and that turned out to be a far better backdrop for a portable turntable than any beach would have been.

## Where the river meets the sea

The wharf sits at the very western edge of Tamsui District in New Taipei City, on the spot where the Tamsui River estuary opens straight into the Taiwan Strait. It started life as a working fishing harbour and was rebuilt into a scenic waterfront around 2001, but the docks still work — you're never far from a real fishing boat. Standing there with a record in your hand, you're not just playing "near the water"; you're playing on the threshold between the island and the open ocean, which is a strange and wonderful feeling.

The landmarks stack up all around you. The centrepiece is Lover's Bridge (情人橋), a sweeping white cable-stayed footbridge designed to look like the mast and rigging of a sailing ship — it lights up in shifting neon colours once the sun drops. Behind it stands Lover's Tower, a hundred-metre rotating observation deck bolted to the Fullon Hotel, like a needle turning slowly over the harbour. A 330-metre elevated wooden boardwalk hugs the shoreline — the same planks the walkers, photographers and buskers all share — and below it the marina bobs with a mix of rugged fishing vessels and sleek private yachts. Across the river mouth, the dark, reclining silhouette of Guanyin Mountain (觀音山) closes off the view, named because its ridgeline looks like the goddess of mercy lying on her back.

## Golden hour, tetrapods, and a crossfader

If you time it for golden hour — and I did — the whole place turns cinematic. The sun sets straight out over the Taiwan Strait, dragging the sky through deep oranges and purples, with Guanyin Mountain going black against it. Underneath my crossfader clicks there was a whole second soundtrack: the heavy, rhythmic slap of waves against the concrete tetrapods (those interlocking four-legged blocks piled along the breakwater), the constant crying of gulls, and the low churn of a ferry engine somewhere off the pier. The air was nothing like central Taipei — no heavy city haze, just sharp sea breeze, a little salt spray, and the briny smell of seafood drying at the vendors. By five o'clock on a weekend the boardwalk had filled up with couples, families and teenagers, and my little turntable became one more thing to slow down and look at.

## Sharing the boardwalk with Tamsui's buskers

Tamsui has one of the liveliest street-performance scenes in Taiwan, and the waterfront is where a lot of it happens. On any given evening you'll share the boardwalk with acoustic guitarists, a saxophonist working through old Taiwanese pop, and — my favourite local touch — uncles belting heavily reverbed karaoke out of the boots of their cars. It's all regulated, though: since March 2021 New Taipei runs a registration-based busking permit rather than the old juried audition, and a licence issued here is honoured across Taipei, Keelung and Taoyuan too. I wasn't collecting money — I was just cutting a few bars for a clip — which lands you in a friendly grey area, so I kept my manners about it. I stayed out of the pinch points on Lover's Bridge, set up near the wider steps by the tetrapods, and kept the volume low enough that I wasn't bleeding into anyone's acoustic set. If security had asked me to move, the plan was simple: smile, say "bù hǎoyìsi," and go.

## The little rig by the water

The setup is the whole reason a place like this is even possible. A Numark PT01 Scratch — a battery-powered portable turntable built for scratch DJs — means I'm never tied to a wall socket, so a boardwalk hanging over a river mouth is as good as any booth. Add a small speaker so the people walking past can actually hear the cuts over the waves, and that's it. A turntable, a record, and a bag you can carry onto the MRT. That portability is the entire point of Scratch the World: if you can carry it, you can play it anywhere — even at a spot you only reached because you were too lazy to go further.

## Why I left a scratch here

I came looking for a surf beach and left a scratch at a river mouth instead, and I wouldn't trade it. That's really what this whole series is about — not the plan, but the place you actually end up, and the little bit of sound you leave behind in it. Tamsui gave me a sunset over the strait, a bridge shaped like a ship, and a soundtrack of waves and gulls to cut against. Not a bad prize for giving up on the beach. Find a spot that means something to you, bring whatever gear you've got, and go leave a scratch of your own.

## Frequently asked questions

### Where is Tamsui Fisherman's Wharf?

Tamsui Fisherman's Wharf (淡水漁人碼頭) is at the western tip of Tamsui District in New Taipei City, Taiwan, where the Tamsui River estuary meets the Taiwan Strait. It's the end of the line up the Taipei MRT Red Line (plus a short bus or ferry hop), and it's known for Lover's Bridge, its long wooden boardwalk, and some of the best sunsets in northern Taiwan.

### Can you perform or busk at Tamsui Fisherman's Wharf?

Yes, but street performing in New Taipei is regulated. Since March 2021 the city uses a registration-based busking permit, and a licence issued in New Taipei is also recognised in Taipei, Keelung and Taoyuan. If you're collecting money or drawing a crowd you need that permit on you; a quiet personal session or a quick video sits in more of a grey area, where keeping the volume down and staying out of the walkways goes a long way.

### What is portablism?

Portablism is scratching with a small, portable turntable instead of full-size decks. It shrinks a DJ rig down to a battery-powered turntable and a fader you can carry anywhere — which is exactly why you can set up on a seaside boardwalk, a park table, or a street corner and start cutting.

### What gear do you need to scratch outdoors by the sea?

Very little: a battery-powered portable turntable like the Numark PT01 Scratch, a scratch record, and a small speaker so passers-by can hear you over the waves. No mixer, no booth, no power outlet — it all fits in a bag you can carry onto the train. Just wipe the salt spray off everything when you're done.
