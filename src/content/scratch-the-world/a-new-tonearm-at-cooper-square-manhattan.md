Stop three on Scratch the World took me back to Manhattan, but this time I had a reason beyond the location. A new tonearm had just arrived for my Numark PT01, and there was really only one place I wanted to test it: Cooper Square, the noisy little crossroads at the top of the Bowery that turntablists know as the longtime home of Scratch DJ Academy. I'm DJ FOOL Wuilin, and here's how the third pin on the map went down — a slightly-down day, a fresh piece of gear, and a patch of downtown pavement that quietly fixed my mood.

## A new tonearm to break in

The whole trip started with a small package. The Numark PT01 Scratch is a battery-powered portable turntable, and one of the first upgrades serious scratch DJs make is swapping the tonearm — the arm that holds the needle — for something that tracks better when you're dragging a record back and forth under the stylus. Mine had just been fitted, and a new tonearm always needs breaking in: you want to feel how it holds the groove, how forgiving it is when you get aggressive, and whether the needle skips when you don't want it to. I'd been having a flat kind of day, honestly — but there's a very specific joy in taking a fresh piece of gear outside to find out what it can do. Scratching with delight even when I was a little down was the whole mood of the afternoon.

## Why Cooper Square

If you don't know it, Cooper Square is a wedge-shaped plaza in downtown Manhattan where the Bowery runs into Third and Fourth Avenues, right on the seam between NoHo and the East Village. It's anchored by The Cooper Union, whose Italianate brownstone Foundation Building has stood there since 1859 — the Great Hall inside is where Abraham Lincoln gave his famous 1860 address. Directly across the street is 41 Cooper Square, Cooper Union's 2009 academic building by architect Thom Mayne, wrapped in a rippling perforated-metal skin that looks like it dropped in from another century. Standing between the two, with traffic roaring off the Bowery and students spilling out of class, you get a very New York collision of old stone and sci-fi metal — a fitting backdrop for a portable turntable that's itself half old-school, half gadget.

## Scratching on hip-hop's home turf

The reason Cooper Square means something to a scratch DJ sits at number 32. For years that address was home to Scratch DJ Academy, the turntablism school co-founded in 2002 by Jam Master Jay of Run-DMC. Generations of DJs learned to cut and mix on this block. Setting up a PT01 on the pavement out front felt less like busking and more like paying quiet respects — you're standing on a stretch of sidewalk where the mechanics of scratching were literally taught, doing the same thing with a turntable small enough to carry in a bag. That lineage is a big part of why Scratch the World keeps coming back to New York.

## The Astor Place cube around the corner

A minute's walk north puts you at Astor Place and the "Alamo" — Tony Rosenthal's black Cor-Ten steel cube, installed in 1967, balanced on a single corner and just about spinnable if a few people lean on it together. It's one of those pieces of public sculpture New Yorkers treat as furniture: skaters carve around its base, students sit under it, tourists take turns giving it a push. Having it around the corner sums up the neighbourhood — art you're allowed to touch, in a place completely relaxed about people doing their own thing in the street. Which is exactly the permission a portablist needs.

## What a new tonearm actually changes

Out on the pavement, the new tonearm did its job. A heavier, better-balanced arm keeps the needle planted when you drag a record backwards at speed, so your baby scratches and cuts come out cleaner and you spend far less time nudging a skipping stylus back into the groove. It's a small mechanical change that makes a real difference to how confident you feel getting aggressive with a sample. By the time I'd cut through a few patterns, the flat mood I'd arrived with had lifted — that's the thing about scratching in public: the city noise, the passers-by, and the simple feedback of a rig that suddenly tracks right all add up to a genuinely good hour.

## Leaving a sound on the Bowery

Looking back, this session was less about a crowd and more about the ritual — new gear, an old block, a reason to go outside. Scratch the World has never been about big stages; it's about carrying a turntable into ordinary, storied places and leaving a little sound behind. Doing that on the same pavement where so many DJs learned the craft felt like the right way to break in a tonearm — and the right way to turn a down day around.

## Frequently asked questions

### Where is Cooper Square in Manhattan?

Cooper Square is a wedge-shaped plaza in downtown Manhattan where the Bowery meets Third and Fourth Avenues, on the border of NoHo and the East Village. It's anchored by The Cooper Union and sits a short walk south of Astor Place and St. Mark's Place.

### What's the connection between Cooper Square and DJ culture?

The address 32 Cooper Square was for years the home of Scratch DJ Academy, the turntablism school co-founded in 2002 by Jam Master Jay of Run-DMC. That made the block a well-known training ground for scratching and mixing, which is why it's a meaningful spot for portablists.

### What is a tonearm on a portable turntable, and why upgrade it?

The tonearm is the arm that holds the needle over the record. On a scratch turntable like the Numark PT01, upgrading to a heavier, better-balanced tonearm helps the needle stay in the groove when you move the record quickly, so your scratches come out cleaner and the stylus skips less.

### Can you scratch on the street at Cooper Square?

Casual, low-volume playing on a busy public plaza like Cooper Square is common and generally fine — the area is full of skaters, students, and street performers. If you want to amplify loudly or draw a crowd, New York City's street-performance and permit rules can apply, so keep it mellow if you're just testing gear.
