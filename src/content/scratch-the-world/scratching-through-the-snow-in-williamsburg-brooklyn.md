On a snowbound, bitterly cold December afternoon in 2017, I carried a battery-powered Numark PT01 Scratch up out of the Bedford Avenue L station and set it down in the slush of Williamsburg, Brooklyn, with the Manhattan skyline flickering in and out of the flurries across the East River. Here is what it is really like to scratch a portable turntable in the middle of a Brooklyn snowstorm.

My caption that day was pure stubbornness: "Extreme weather also can't stop me." Four days earlier I had cut records in the strange, shifting wind out at Hudson Yards and told myself the weather couldn't stop me; Williamsburg decided to test that claim with actual snow. I went anyway.

## A neighbourhood built on cold brick and vinyl

By December 2017, Williamsburg had long finished its turn from a working industrial corner of Brooklyn into the place everyone pointed at when they said the word "hipster" — specialty coffee, record stores, boutiques, and new glass towers rising over the old low brick. What I like about it as a place to scratch is that the grit never fully washed out. Step one block off Bedford Avenue and there are still graffiti-tagged loading docks, mural-covered alleyways, and the run of brick warehouses around the Brooklyn Brewery on North 11th Street. It is a neighbourhood built on cold brick, and it has never once stopped respecting a good drum break.

## Up the stairs at the Bedford Avenue L

The Bedford Avenue L stop is the front door to North Williamsburg, and the wide concrete apron at the top of its stairs is where every busker in the neighbourhood eventually ends up. You come up the steps into the main artery of Bedford Avenue — commuters, art-school kids and weekend visitors all funnelling past — and if you set up just clear of the stair mouth, out of everyone's path, you have the best foot traffic in the borough. I planted the PT01 on a flat ledge a few steps off the corner, careful not to block the subway grate or anyone's doorway, and warmed my hands over the records before the first cut.

## Snow on the cobblestones, the skyline behind the flurries

The snow that afternoon was the wet, heavy kind that turns the cobblestones dark and the asphalt to grey slush, then black under the streetlamps as the early-December light gave out around half past four. I walked down toward the East River to look. In late 2017 that waterfront was still raw — the old Domino Sugar Refinery sitting behind construction fences with its gantry cranes and rusted steel, the park that would later soften all of it still half a year from opening. From the water's edge the Manhattan skyline came and went through the flurries, lit amber by storefront Edison bulbs and the neon of the bodegas behind me. The air smelled of coal smoke and roasting espresso and wet wool, and every few minutes the elevated J, M and Z trains rattled across the overpass toward the bridge with a long metallic rumble.

## Scratching under the green bridge

Down in South Williamsburg the Williamsburg Bridge throws its towering green steel trusses over the streets, and the trains crossing it keep the whole frame of the neighbourhood humming. Scratching out there means scratching against all of it at once — the rumble of the elevated trains, the hum of traffic on the bridge, the hiss of tyres through slush. A little battery speaker does not win that fight on volume, so I did not try. In the cold my fingers went stiff within minutes, so I leaned away from fast flares and crabs and into heavier tears and slow baby scratches — the cuts I could still land when I could not feel my hands — shielding the platter with a glove so a gust would not send the needle skating off a boom-bap instrumental.

## A crowd that only stops for something real

Williamsburg does not stop for buskers out of politeness. In freezing cold, people walk with their collars up and their minds made up, and a generic Top 40 loop would have earned nothing here but a wider berth. What works is the real thing — 90s boom-bap, a drum break juggled by hand, honest scratching — and when I got a run going, a few people actually stopped: a couple sharing one umbrella, a guy holding a coffee, a kid who plainly knew exactly what a crab scratch was. A couple of bills landed in the bag. In a neighbourhood this proud of its underground, catching even three strangers in the falling snow felt like the entire point of coming out.

## Frequently asked questions

### Where did you scratch on 10 December 2017?

In Williamsburg, Brooklyn — up around the Bedford Avenue L stop in North Williamsburg, with the snow coming down over Bedford Avenue, the raw East River waterfront and the old Domino Sugar Refinery off to the west, and the green steel of the Williamsburg Bridge down in the south of the neighbourhood.

### Isn't snow the worst possible weather to bring a turntable out in?

It is close to the worst, yes. Wet snow and a battery turntable are not natural friends: the plastic chassis goes stiff and cold, your fingers stop cooperating within minutes, and slush finds its way into everything. The way through is to keep the set-up fast, tuck out of the wind and the falling snow under an overhang, wipe the platter and your hands often, and swap the fiddly fast patterns for heavier, slower cuts you can still land with numb fingers.

### How do you power a turntable outdoors in Williamsburg?

Entirely off its own batteries. There are no outdoor outlets to go hunting for on a snowy Brooklyn sidewalk, which is exactly why a battery-powered deck like the PT01 exists — it runs on internal power or D-cells, and a small battery amp or speaker is what lets it cut through the traffic and the trains. Keep the footprint small, stay clear of storefront doorways and the subway grates, and the NYPD generally leaves a quiet, low-impact busker alone as long as you are not blocking the sidewalk.

### What gear did you use?

A Numark PT01 Scratch — a portable, battery-powered turntable — paired with a wireless Mixfader. The whole rig lives in a shoulder bag and sets up on a ledge in under a minute, which is the only reason bringing it out into a snowstorm is even an option.
