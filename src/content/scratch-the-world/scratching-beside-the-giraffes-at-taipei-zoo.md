The caption I posted that day was pure nonsense, and I still love it: "Giraffe, the peeper XDDDD." A giraffe had leaned its long neck out over the savanna and seemed to peer straight down at the little turntable, and that was the whole joke. A week into scratching my way around Taipei I'd already hit a temple and a gadget mall, so why not a zoo?

## Off the Brown Line and into the trees

Taipei Zoo sits at the very end of the Wenhu (Brown) Line, and you feel the city loosen its grip as the train climbs out toward Muzha. This is Wenshan District, the green, hilly southern edge of Taipei where the Jingmei River winds past and the Maokong tea hills rise straight up behind the park. Step off at the elevated terminus and the Maokong Gondola station is right there by the gates, its cables humming a low mechanical note out over the valley. It was September, so the air was thick and wet — the kind of subtropical humidity that promises an afternoon downpour — and the walk in from the entrance is a gentle, sweaty incline, because the whole zoo is built into the forested foothills rather than laid out flat.

## Asia's biggest zoo, built into a jungle

People forget how enormous this place is. Taipei Zoo spans about 165 hectares, the largest zoo in Asia, and it's so heavily wooded that it feels less like a concrete city zoo and more like a jungle someone carved walkways into. It opened on this Muzha site back in 1986, and past the entrance plaza the first stretch is the Formosan Animal Area, all native Taiwanese species — black bears, macaques and sika deer set behind deep moats and dense local greenery. I drifted past the Giant Panda House too, which even then was the single most congested bottleneck in the park: timed-entry tickets, a wall of people, exactly the kind of spot you do not try to set up a turntable. So I kept walking, deeper and uphill, toward the wide-open African zone.

## Finding a spot by the giraffes

The African Animal Area is a six-hectare stretch of pretend East African savanna — tall grasses, an artificial watering hole, zebras and giraffes sharing one big open habitat with the green Taiwanese hills rising behind them. I found a length of smooth wooden handrail near the giraffe viewing deck, balanced the PT01 on it, and dropped the needle. The soundscape was gloriously chaotic: summer cicadas buzzing at that deafening Taipei volume, school groups filing past chattering in Mandarin and Taiwanese Hokkien, the odd squawk drifting over from another enclosure. Dappled sun came through the broad tropical leaves, my hands were sweating enough to make the vinyl feel sticky, and the whole place smelled of dry dust, a little herbivore manure, damp earth from the morning's rain and the sweet tang of shaved ice from a vendor cart. Then the giraffe leaned over to look — and that was the shot.

## Cutting a scratch completely off the books

None of this was allowed, and it's worth being straight about that. Street performance in Taiwan is a licensed activity: since the early 2000s Taipei's buskers (街頭藝人) have had to audition for a permit from the city's Department of Cultural Affairs and stick to designated zones like Ximending and the Xinyi shopping district. A city-run family zoo is emphatically not on that map, which makes a turntable on the giraffe railing pure guerrilla work. The etiquette that makes it possible is all about restraint: keep the little internal speaker low, both so it doesn't spook the giraffes — they're sensitive to sudden noise — and so it doesn't carry to a roaming zookeeper; keep your footprint tiny so you're never blocking a stroller; and be ready to smile, murmur a soft "bù hǎoyìsi" (excuse me), and melt back into the crowd the moment a uniform rounds the corner. Taiwanese crowds are polite and curious — families slowed down, kids pointed at the spinning 7-inch — and the trick is simply to stay as gentle and low-key as the place itself.

## Why a zoo was the perfect Taiwan stop

Scratch the World has always been about carrying a turntable into ordinary, unexpected, in-between places and leaving a little sound behind. A wholesome civic day out — families, school uniforms, shaved ice, giraffes — is about the last place anyone expects a hip-hop crossfader, and that clash is the whole reason I love it. Everything still fit in one bag slung over a shoulder, ready to appear for ten minutes and vanish again. The Taipei map was filling in fast, and the giraffe, as far as I'm concerned, gave it a nod of approval.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can you busk or DJ inside Taipei Zoo?

Not officially. Street performance in Taiwan is licensed — buskers (街頭藝人) audition for a permit from the city's Department of Cultural Affairs and perform only in designated public zones — and a city-run zoo is a formal, family attraction that sits outside all of that. The only realistic way to do anything like this is quietly and briefly: keep the volume low, stay out of the walkways, and pack up the moment anyone official asks.

### What is Taipei Zoo?

Taipei Zoo (臺北市立動物園) is the largest zoo in Asia, spanning around 165 hectares in the Muzha area of Wenshan District in southern Taipei. It opened on its current site in 1986, sits at the terminus of the MRT Wenhu (Brown) Line with the Maokong Gondola station right next door, and is known for its Giant Panda House and its six-hectare African savanna area, where the giraffes live.

### Is it hard to scratch on a portable turntable near animals?

The hard part is the gear and the volume. A battery portable like the Numark PT01 has a light platter and a light tonearm, so aggressive cuts can knock the needle out of the groove, and here you also have to keep the internal speaker quiet — loud enough to hear, soft enough not to startle the giraffes or draw a zookeeper. You end up scratching smaller, cleaner and more carefully than you would on a full-size deck.

### What gear did you use?

A Numark PT01 Scratch — a battery-powered portable turntable built for scratch DJs — with a portable fader and a scratch record. Because it runs on batteries and fits in a shoulder bag, it sets up on a railing anywhere, which is the only reason a session inside a Taipei zoo is even possible.
