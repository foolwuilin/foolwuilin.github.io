Not every stop on Scratch the World is a street corner. This one is a granite plaza the size of a small neighbourhood — a place Taiwan literally renamed for freedom. In late September 2017 I hauled my little portable turntable out to Liberty Square, the vast open space in front of Chiang Kai-shek Memorial Hall in Taipei, and cut a few bars under that famous blue octagonal roof. I'm DJ FOOL Wuilin (吳奕霖), and this is the stop I still think of as my "Freedom Square instead of the Statue of Liberty" moment.

## Freedom Square instead of the Statue of Liberty

When I posted the clip, I captioned it "Freedom Square instead of the Statue of Liberty" — and standing there, that felt exactly right. Chiang Kai-shek Memorial Hall anchors the eastern end of the plaza: a stark white marble monument capped by a brilliant blue octagonal roof, reached by a flight of exactly 89 steps (the number is Chiang Kai-shek's age when he died). It was built as a solemn tribute to a strongman. Then in 2007 the square around it was officially renamed 自由廣場 — Liberty Square — turning a monument to authority into an open stage for public expression. Scratching is an art form built entirely on free expression, so setting a turntable down here felt less like a stunt and more like the place and the music agreeing on something.

## Why Liberty Square is made for street scratching

It's one of the biggest open public spaces in the whole city, and getting there with a bag of gear is almost too easy. You ride the Taipei MRT to Chiang Kai-Shek Memorial Hall station — served by the Red (Tamsui–Xinyi) and Green (Songshan–Xindian) lines — and if you come up Exit 5 you basically surface right at the edge of the plaza, no dragging equipment through traffic. The main gate is a huge blue-and-white archway facing Zhongshan South Road, with "Liberty Square" spelled out in flowing calligraphy modelled on the style of the master Wang Xizhi. Walk through it and the whole expanse opens up in front of you: the white hall dead ahead, and the National Theater and National Concert Hall facing each other across the square in perfect mirror symmetry, their golden-yellow palace roofs and red pillars glowing.

## Where the dancers gather — and where I set up

Here's the local knowledge you only get by showing up: almost nobody performs out in the bare middle of the plaza. The wind rips straight across that flat granite with nothing to slow it down, and it'll bump your tonearm off the record in a heartbeat. The real action lives under the covered walkways of the National Theater. Every afternoon and into the evening, teenage hip-hop crews, K-pop cover dancers, and martial-arts practitioners claim their patches of smooth tile there, using the theater's tall reflective glass doors as mirrors. Those deep under-hangs cut the wind and turn into little acoustic pockets — the ideal spot to set a portable turntable down. It all runs on an unspoken, first-come, first-served respect: everyone carves out their own zone, and you keep your volume from stomping on the next crew's boombox. (Out of courtesy to the memorial's guards, nobody blasts music up on the 89 steps or by the hall itself.)

## The little rig that travels with me

It's the same bag I always carry. A Numark PT01 Scratch — a battery-powered portable turntable built for scratch DJs, so I'm never chained to a wall socket. A Mixfader, the wireless Bluetooth crossfader that gives you real, responsive cuts. And a small speaker so the people walking past can actually hear the cuts over the open plaza. That's the entire point of portablism: the whole setup fits in a backpack you can carry down into the MRT and back up Exit 5, and suddenly a piece of ground like this becomes a stage.

## What it feels like to scratch under the blue roof

Cutting out there is nothing like a booth. By day the white marble throws the sun straight back at you and the humid Taipei heat just sits on the stone. Come toward dusk the light softens, the blue roof deepens against the sky, and the plaza fills with echoing chatter, the distant hum of city traffic, and — on the hour — the sharp, synchronized stomp of the changing of the guards drifting down from the hall. You learn to lock your rhythm into all of it. People slow down, a few stop, and someone always asks if that's really a record. Your hands keep moving while one of the most loaded pieces of ground in Taiwan moves around you.

## Why I scratch the world

That's the whole idea behind this map: a tourist takes a photo to remember a place; I leave a scratch instead. Liberty Square was one of my favourite places to leave a sound behind, precisely because of everything it means — a space named for freedom, full of kids expressing themselves, with room enough for one more person and a little turntable. ## Frequently asked questions

### Can you play music or busk at Chiang Kai-shek Memorial Hall?

Yes — Liberty Square is a lively public space where street performers gather daily, especially under the covered walkways of the National Theater along the plaza's edge. As a courtesy (and to stay on good terms with the memorial's guards), performers keep loud music off the 89 steps and away from the main hall, and keep the volume neighbourly with the dance crews sharing the space.

### What is Liberty Square, and why was it renamed?

Liberty Square (自由廣場) is the large plaza surrounding Chiang Kai-shek Memorial Hall in Taipei. In 2007 it was officially renamed from "Chiang Kai-shek Memorial Hall Plaza" to Liberty Square, reflecting Taiwan's democratic transformation. Its main gate carries the "Liberty Square" inscription in calligraphy modelled on the style of Wang Xizhi.

### How do you get to Liberty Square?

Take the Taipei MRT to Chiang Kai-Shek Memorial Hall station, served by the Red (Tamsui–Xinyi) and Green (Songshan–Xindian) lines. Exit 5 brings you up right at the edge of the plaza, which is handy when you're carrying gear.

### What is portablism?

Portablism is scratching on a small, portable turntable instead of full-size decks. It shrinks a DJ rig down to a little turntable, a fader, and a speaker you can carry anywhere — which is exactly why you can set up on a plaza like Liberty Square and just start cutting.
