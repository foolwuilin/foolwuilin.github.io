On 30 October 2017, four of us carried battery-powered Numark PT01 Scratch turntables across the Hudson to the Hoboken waterfront in New Jersey, lined up along the railing, and passed cuts down the line with the Manhattan skyline spread out in front of us. My caption that day was about the weather — "yesterday is windy, better than today though" — which tells you exactly what kind of spot this is: right out on the open river, where the wind gets a vote. Most weekends our crew was scratching in New York parks. This time we'd crossed the water just to look back at the city.

## Across the river from the city we usually play

Hoboken sits directly across the Hudson from Lower Manhattan, and its waterfront is a string of parks built right out over the water. We set up around Pier A Park, a big green space that juts into the river with a Great Lawn edged by a concrete promenade and rows of mature London plane trees. When you drop a needle here, you're looking straight across at the West Village and the Financial District — the same towers that sit behind you when you play in a Manhattan park, except now they're the whole horizon.

For a crew that spent most weekends cutting in New York, it was a funny feeling. We'd driven out of the city, crossed the river, and set our decks down just to face it from the other side. A few weeks earlier we'd done the same thing on a hill at Lambert Castle; this time the city wasn't a thin grey line in the distance, it was right there, close enough to read the buildings.

## A windy afternoon on the Hudson

The river works like a wind tunnel, so any crossbreeze off the water hits sharp and carries that briny, slightly diesel-tinged smell of the Hudson and the passing ferries. The late-October sun was already sitting low, throwing long golden-hour shadows down the brick walkway and bouncing a hard glare off the glass towers across the water. My caption joked that the day before had been worse; out there, "a bit less windy" is genuinely a good report.

The soundtrack is dense. The slap of water against the wooden pilings, the low hum of ferry engines pulling in and out, the rhythmic thud of joggers, stroller wheels rattling over the concrete, and every so often a helicopter tracing the river corridor overhead. Four little PT01 speakers ticking and chirping had to find their own gap in all of that, which is part of why a windy waterfront is such an honest place to play — the river doesn't give you any room to hide a sloppy cut.

## Four portables, passing it down the line

The whole point of the day was playing together. My caption lists the order we ran it: @_justdj_, then me, then @talamarc, then @djcplex_nyc. We passed it like a tag-team routine — each person takes a short phrase and hands the beat down the line to the next deck. With portables, nobody is "on the decks" while everyone else waits; you're all sitting in a row, each with your own turntable, listening and answering.

On the PT01 there's no mixer channel to hide behind. The built-in speaker is small, so you lean in, and everyone hears exactly what you just did. Someone throws a quick chirp, the next person answers with a flare, and by the fourth deck the pattern has turned into something none of us planned. Out on the open waterfront, with the wind eating half the volume, you end up cutting a little harder just to be heard down the line.

## Hoboken's waterfront, stage by stage

If you come to do this yourself, the waterfront is really a series of distinct spots, each with a slightly different backdrop. Pier A Park anchors the lower end, with its wide lawn and the cleanest, most open skyline view. Walk north along Frank Sinatra Drive and you reach Sinatra Park, where concrete steps step down toward the water and make a natural place to sit and set up a deck.

The copper-clad, Beaux-Arts Hoboken Terminal marks the southern end, feeding the whole strip with commuters off the PATH train, NJ Transit, and the NY Waterway ferry. Keep going north and you hit Pier C Park, built out on its own island structure with a striking modern fishing pier, then Maxwell Place — a former Maxwell House coffee plant turned waterfront park — and finally Castle Point at Stevens Institute of Technology, the highest natural point in town, looming over the river. A few blocks inland, Washington Street runs parallel to all of it: brownstones, old Italian bakeries, and third-wave coffee shops if you need to warm up after the wind.

## A town that's always had music

Hoboken wears its music history openly. Frank Sinatra was born here — there's a bronze plaque at his birth site at 415 Monroe Street, and the waterfront road is literally named Frank Sinatra Drive. Setting up a turntable on this stretch of concrete feels less like an intrusion and more like adding one more small, modern layer to a town that has been making public music for a very long time. It has the density and energy of a Brooklyn block with the fierce local pride of an old New Jersey port town, and on a Sunday afternoon four people with tiny record players fit right into the mix of joggers, dog-walkers and skyline photographers.

## How I'd set up on a public waterfront like this

Hoboken's waterfront is a heavily used, heavily residential public space, so we kept it low-key:

**Batteries only.** There are no outlets out on the piers, and you don't want to go hunting for one. The PT01 runs on batteries, which is exactly why it suits a spot like this.

**Keep it a personal monitor, not a PA.** The local rule of thumb is that your sound shouldn't be plainly audible far away, so keep the speaker at a volume that's really just for you and the person next to you — not for the whole park.

**Use the lawns and the steps, not the choke points.** The Pier A lawn and the Sinatra Park steps are roomy and fine; avoid blocking the narrow parts of the walkway or setting up directly under the residential windows further up the waterfront.

**Turn it down the moment someone asks.** If a noise officer or the police ask you to lower it, do it right away. Permits are really meant for formal, amplified performances, so staying low-profile is the whole strategy.

**Share the space.** Joggers, strollers and photographers all want that skyline too. Shift over and give them the shot.

**Carry everything out.** Spare batteries, cables, slipmats, sleeves. Leave the railing exactly how you found it.

## Frequently asked questions

### Where's the best view of the Manhattan skyline in Hoboken?

Pier A Park has the widest, most open view straight across at Lower Manhattan, and Sinatra Park just to the north is a close second with steps down to the water. Both sit right on the Hudson, so you're looking at the West Village and Financial District across the river.

### Can you play music on the Hoboken waterfront?

It's a gray area. A small, battery-powered, personal-monitor-volume session on the lawn or the steps, away from residential windows, is generally fine and nobody bothered us. If a park officer or the police ask you to turn it down, pack it in — formal amplified performances are the thing that technically needs a permit.

### How do you get to the Hoboken waterfront?

The easiest way from Manhattan is the PATH train or the NY Waterway ferry into Hoboken Terminal, then a short walk to the water. NJ Transit also runs into the same terminal, and from anywhere in town you just walk east down Washington Street until you hit the river.

### What gear did you use?

A battery-powered Numark PT01 Scratch portable turntable, a record and headphones. The other three DJs brought their own portables, so it was four decks in a row passing cuts down the line.
