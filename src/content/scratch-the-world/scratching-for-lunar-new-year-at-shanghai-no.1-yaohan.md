By late January, Pudong had gone full New Year. I'd spent the winter in Shanghai making beats, and I wanted one stop on the map that felt like the city at its loudest and its reddest. So I picked the plaza in front of Shanghai No.1 Yaohan — the giant department store locals just call 八佰伴 (Bābǎibàn) — set my little turntable on a bench, and cut a few bars into the cold while shoppers streamed past with their arms full of gift boxes. I'm DJ FOOL Wuilin (吳奕霖). This is the Shanghai pin.

## A department store with a surprising backstory

Shanghai No.1 Yaohan (上海第一八佰伴) isn't just any mall. It opened in late 1995 as a Sino-Japanese joint venture between the Shanghai Baolian Group and Japan's Yaohan — China's first big foreign-invested department store, and for years it carried the "Nextage" name. It's enormous: around 140,000 square metres over ten floors, wrapped in a sweeping concave glass facade with a soaring atrium and zig-zagging escalators you can read from the street. It sits right on the corner of Zhangyang Road and South Pudong Road, deep in Pudong's old-school retail district — a different world from the Lujiazui skyscrapers a few blocks northwest, even though the Shanghai Tower and Jin Mao glitter on the skyline behind you. Coming by metro, Shangcheng Road station on Line 9 drops you about a five-minute walk south.

## Setting up under the Year of the Tiger arches

2022 was the Year of the Tiger, and the plaza outside had been dressed for it — big red-and-gold festive arches, tiered LED ribbons, and paper lanterns strung from the bare winter trees along Zhangyang Road. Late January in Shanghai is a damp, cutting kind of cold, somewhere around 4–8°C, the sort that turns your breath to vapour and stiffens your fingertips the moment they touch a crossfader. I kept my hands buried in my pockets until the last possible second, because cold fingers lose the fine control a fader needs almost instantly. When the light dropped, the whole square lit gold and neon, the glow smearing across the damp granite pavers.

## The little rig that travels with me

The setup is the same one I carry everywhere: a Numark PT01 Scratch — a battery-powered portable turntable built for scratch DJs, so I'm never hunting for a wall socket — plus a small speaker so people walking past can actually hear the cuts. That's the whole point of portablism: the entire kit fits in a shoulder bag and rides the metro with you, which is exactly how a scratch session ends up on a bench outside a Shanghai department store on a freezing pre-holiday night.

## What it actually feels like out there

Scratching here means cutting against the full soundtrack of Pudong at rush hour. Blue Ele.me and yellow Meituan delivery scooters purr and beep as they thread through the crowd, buses hiss at the kerb, and Yaohan's outdoor speakers pipe cheerful Lunar New Year brass standards — you haven't really lived until you're trying to hold a rhythm over a PA blasting "Gong Xi Fa Cai." The air smells of chestnuts roasting in hot black grit, sweet potatoes baking on a cart wheeled up to an alley mouth, and warm butter venting from the bakeries down in the basement food hall. The crowd is pure Shanghai New Year: older locals carrying red boxes of cured meats and Shaoxing wine, and streetwear kids drifting out of the mall across the road with boba cups tucked into insulated sleeves. Most people don't cheer — they slow down, film a quick fifteen-second clip for Douyin, murmur "hěn kù" (pretty cool), and move on. Reserved, curious, and very much on their phones.

## Scratching in Shanghai without making a scene

Here's the honest part: Shanghai is not a drop-a-hat busking city. Street-performance permits do exist, but they're mostly confined to designated cultural strips like West Nanjing Road, and a commercial plaza like this one answers to the mall's own management and the district urban-management team. Uniformed security (bǎo'ān) patrol constantly, so a portable session here runs on a friendly hit-and-run rhythm: set up on a bench, keep the volume modest, smile, and treat it as a casual jam rather than a concert, and you usually get a good ten or fifteen minutes before a guard wanders over with a polite "what are you up to?" and asks you to pack it in. One rule I never break — no hat, no open case, no payment QR code. The instant it looks like you're collecting money, a casual jam becomes unlicensed soliciting, and that's a much shorter conversation. So I cut my bars, grinned at the guard, and slid the PT01 back into the bag. Pin dropped.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is Shanghai No.1 Yaohan?

Shanghai No.1 Yaohan (上海第一八佰伴), once branded Nextage, is a landmark department store in Pudong that opened in 1995 as China's first large foreign-invested department store — a joint venture with Japan's Yaohan group. It's roughly 140,000 square metres over ten floors, on the corner of Zhangyang Road and South Pudong Road, and it's famous for going all-out with its Lunar New Year decorations.

### Can you busk or scratch on the street in Shanghai?

Not freely. Shanghai runs official street-performance permits, but they're limited to designated cultural zones, and commercial plazas are controlled by private security and the district urban-management bureau. A quiet, short portable session treated as a casual jam is usually tolerated for a few minutes — but the moment you solicit money it becomes an enforcement issue. Keep it low-key and low-volume.

### What gear do you need to scratch outdoors?

Not much: a battery-powered portable turntable like the Numark PT01 Scratch, a scratch record, and a small speaker so passers-by can hear you. No mixer, no booth, no power outlet — it all fits in a bag. In winter, add warm pockets, because cold fingers kill your fader control fast.

### What is portablism?

Portablism is scratching on a small, portable turntable instead of full-size decks. Shrinking the rig down to something you can carry is exactly what lets you set up on a park bench, a street corner, or a plaza outside a Shanghai department store and just start cutting.
