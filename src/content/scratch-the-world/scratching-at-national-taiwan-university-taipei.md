On 16 September 2017 I carried a battery-powered Numark PT01 onto the campus of National Taiwan University — 台大, the most famous university in the country — and cut a few bars under the Royal Palms while the cicadas screamed and students rolled past on their bikes. This is what it's like to scratch a portable turntable inside Taiwan's most prestigious campus at the sticky tail-end of a subtropical summer: humid, unhurried, and completely off the tourist script.

The caption I posted that day was simple and a little proud: "The top 1 University in Taiwan, National Taiwan University. Had a good talk with @jerry920181 : )." Both parts were true. I really was standing on the grounds of Taiwan's number-one university — and the best part of the afternoon wasn't the scratching at all. It was sitting and talking with a friend between takes, in the shade, with the whole green campus breathing around us.

## The most famous campus in Taiwan

National Taiwan University — 國立臺灣大學, universally shortened to Taida (台大) — is the oldest and most prestigious university in the country, and its main campus spreads across Da'an District on the south side of Taipei. It works like a green lung for the city: block after block of Japanese colonial-era red-brick buildings, wide lawns, old trees, and more open sky than you get almost anywhere else in central Taipei. Wrapped right around it is Gongguan (公館), Taipei's quintessential university town — a neon-lit labyrinth of narrow alleys packed with independent bookshops, cheap food stalls, bubble-tea counters and underground live houses. Stepping between the two, from the noise of Gongguan into the calm of campus, is half the experience.

## In through the gate on Roosevelt Road

I came in the way most people do, through the fortress-like red-brick main gate on Roosevelt Road Section 4 (羅斯福路). The moment you pass under it, the chaos of Gongguan traffic drops away and the campus opens up in front of you. Straight ahead runs 椰林大道 — Palm Boulevard, the spine of the whole place: a wide, dead-straight avenue flanked by towering, decades-old Royal Palm trees and azalea bushes, aimed like an arrow at the grand old Main Library. Off to one side stands the Fu Bell (傅鐘), the symbol of the school, which rings 21 times a day — a nod to the idea that a person has 21 hours for living and learning and should keep the other 3 for reflection. Nearby, tucked into dense trees, Fu Garden (傅園) hides a little Greek-style pavilion. It is a campus that takes its own symbolism seriously, and it earns it.

## A sticky September afternoon

September in Taipei is not autumn — it's the long tail of a subtropical summer. The air sat around 30°C and thick enough that I was sweating just standing still, the sky doing its usual late-summer trick of shifting from hazy blue toward bruised purple as an afternoon storm thought about rolling in. The soundtrack was pure Taipei summer: the deafening, metallic shriek of cicadas pouring out of the banyan trees, loud enough to sit right on top of a beat, and under it the constant whir of hundreds of bicycle chains and tyres on asphalt as students crossed the huge campus the only sensible way. Sunlight came down through the palm fronds in sharp, dappled patches on the hot pavement; banyan aerial roots hung like curtains over the brick walls. Every so often the wind carried in the smell of scallion pancakes and the pungent tang of stinky tofu from the Gongguan market, mixed with wet soil and a little traffic exhaust. In the middle of all of it, the good talk with @jerry920181 that the caption mentioned — the kind of unhurried afternoon a campus is built for.

## Setting up the PT01 on a campus bench

Finding a spot was easy: a stone bench in the shade, a stack of 7-inch records, and the little Numark PT01 balanced on top. And then, as usual, the deck humbled me. A light portable turntable on an uneven surface, sweaty hands, and cicadas competing for the high end — my cuts that afternoon were rough, and I knew it. But that's the whole point of doing this in public: you set up somewhere real and you make the sound anyway. Students slowed their bikes to watch the record move, a few discreetly lifted a phone to grab a clip, and when I caught someone's eye and smiled I got a shy, polite nod back. Nobody stopped me. On a campus like this, a lone guy with a tiny turntable reads as a hobby or a club practising, not a disturbance.

## Why a university campus earns a place on the map

It might sound strange to scratch at a university rather than a landmark, but Taida is exactly the right kind of place. Its plazas run on do-it-yourself practice culture — hip-hop dance crews rehearsing in the reflections of glass doors, guitar clubs strumming on the steps, martial-arts groups drilling in the open. A portable-turntable session slots straight into that communal, self-taught energy. And just outside the gate, Gongguan has been the beating heart of Taipei's underground and indie music scene for years, home to some of the city's most iconic live houses. So a scratch on the NTU lawns wasn't as out of place as it looks — it was two sides of the same neighbourhood, the elite campus and the raw street culture, meeting on a bench for an afternoon.

## Frequently asked questions

### Where was this filmed?

On the main campus of National Taiwan University (台大) in Da'an District, Taipei — in through the red-brick main gate on Roosevelt Road Section 4, near the Royal Palms of Palm Boulevard (椰林大道) and a short walk from the Gongguan (公館) neighbourhood.

### Can you just set up and scratch on the NTU campus?

Mostly, yes — with common sense. Taipei runs a formal "Street Artist" permit system (街頭藝人) for buskers who want to collect tips in designated city spots, but the NTU campus itself is open to the public. As long as you're not putting out a tip jar, blocking a walkway, or running a big PA that disrupts classes, campus security will generally leave you alone; a small battery-powered turntable reads as personal practice rather than a performance.

### What is Gongguan, and why does it matter for music?

Gongguan is the university town wrapped around NTU — dense alleys of bookshops, food stalls and bubble tea, and, more importantly, the epicentre of Taipei's underground and indie music scene, home to iconic live houses. It's the kind of neighbourhood where a portable turntable never feels far from home.

### What gear did you use?

A battery-powered Numark PT01 Scratch — the same little portable deck I've carried through this whole series, chosen because it runs on batteries and fits in a bag, so any bench can become a booth.
